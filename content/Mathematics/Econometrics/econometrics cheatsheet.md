
### Fundamentals

- The **Data Generating Process** (**DGP**) is the combined economic and measurement process that yields the observed economic data. For example, a DGP might involve the purchase decisions of many individual buyers across multiple stores, combined with how those sales are recorded.
- An **econometric model** is a concise and reasonably accurate reflection of the data generating process. By disregarding less relevant aspects of the data, the model helps us better understand the DGP's main features.
- The **true model** is an idealized concept reflecting a situation where an econometric model perfectly describes the DGP. Because an econometric model is always an approximation, a literal "true model" does not make much practical sense. However, it is a helpful mathematical fiction used to analyze the statistical properties of estimation methods.

### Linear Model

The true **linear model** postulates that a dependent variable $y$ depends linearly on unknown parameters $\beta$, and explanatory variables $X$, plus some unobserved disturbance term $\epsilon$. This model is expressed in matrix form as:

$$
y = X\beta+\epsilon
$$

In practice we obtain a finite sample of data that has been generated by this underlying process. From this process we actually observe:
- $y$: an $n \times 1$ vector of observations for the dependent variable.
- $X$ an $n \times k$ matrix of observations of the explanatory variables.

We do not know the underlying parameters of the true model. Both the $k \times 1$ parameter vector $\beta$, and the $n \times 1$ vector of random disturbances $\epsilon$ remain completely unknown.

The primary task in econometric analysis is to estimate these parameters as accurately as possible using our observed data $X$ and $y$. If $b$ is a $k \times 1$ vector of estimates for the true parameters $\beta$, the estimated model is written as:

$$
y = Xb+e
$$

Here, $e$ denotes the $n \times 1$ vector of **residuals**, representing the observable estimation errors, calculated as the difference between our actual data and the fitted values $e = y - Xb$. Furthermore, the predicted values may be denoted as $\hat{y}$ (pronounced "y-hat"), and calculated as $\hat{y}=Xb$. Then the residuals are simply $e = y - \hat{y}$.

### Loss Functions

When fitting a model to data, a criterion (or loss) function evaluates the fit by penalizing deviations.

- **Quadratic Loss (Least Squares)** is the most frequently used criterion, measuring the sum of squared deviations, $S(b)=\sum_{i}e_{i}^2 =e'e=(y-Xb)'(y-Xb)$. This method is commonly referred to as **ordinary least squares** (**OLS**).
- **Absolute Loss** is an alternative function that relies on the absolute values of the deviations, $S_{abs}(b)=\sum_{i}|e_{i}|$.
- **Huber's Loss** combines the best properties of both quadratic and absolute loss. It is quadratic for small errors (where $|r_{i}| \le \delta$) making it differentiable at zero, and linear for large errors (where $|r_{i}| > \delta$), making it more robust to outliers, $S_{h}(b)=\sum_{i}L_{\delta}(e_{i})$
    $$
    L_{\delta}(a) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2}(a)^2 & \text{if } |a| \leq \delta \\ \delta \left(|a| - \frac{\delta}{2}\right) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
    $$

From here on, we will only be concerned with minimizing the quadratic loss. 

### Coefficient of Determination $R^2$

To find the vector of estimates $b$ that minimizes the quadratic loss we set the first-order derivatives of the loss function $S(b)$ to zero, and solve for $b$. This results in the least square estimator $b = (X'X)^{-1}X'y$.

The performance of the least squares method can be evaluated by decomposing the total sample variation.
- **Total Sum of Squares** (**SST**): the total variation in the dependent variable $y$, calculated as $SST = \sum_{i}(y_{i}-\overline{y})^2$. 
- **Explained Sum of Squares** (**SSE**): the portion of the variation that is explained by the model. $SSE=\sum_{i}(\hat{y}_{i}-\overline{y})^2$
- **Sum of Squared Residuals** (**SSR**): the unexplained, residual part of the variation, calculated as $SSR = \sum_{i} e_{i}^2 = e'e$.
- Relation: $SST = SSE + SSR$.

The **coefficient of determination**, denoted by $R^2$ (R-squared), measures the fraction of the total sample variation that is explained by the model. It is defined as the relative explained sum of squares: $R^2=\frac{SSE}{SST}=1-\frac{SSR}{SST}$. Minimizing the least squares criterion is equivalent to maximizing $R^2$. If the model contains a constant (intercept) term, then $0 \leq R^2 \leq 1$. If not, then $SSR$ may be larger than $SST$, making $R^2 < 0$.

When explanatory variables are added to a model, $R^2$ value never decreases. However, this does not necessarily mean the model is improving in a meaningful way. This motivates the **adjusted** $R^2$ ($\bar{R}^2$) to adjust for the degrees of freedom $k$ (the number of explanatory variables + 1 for the intercept) using the following formula:
$$
\overline{R}^2 =  1-\frac{n-1}{n-k}(1-R^2)
$$

### Seven Assumptions

The rather strict conditions outlined in the seven assumptions are introduced to simplify the analytical proofs and allow for mathematically exact results when determining the statistical properties and accuracy of least squares estimation.

- **Assumption 1: fixed regressors.** All elements of the $n \times k$ matrix $X$ containing the observations on the explanatory variables are non-stochastic. It is assumed that $n \ge k$ and that the matrix $X$ has rank $k$.
- **Assumption 2: random disturbances, zero mean.** The $n \times 1$ vector $\epsilon$ consists of random disturbances with a mean of zero, meaning $E[\epsilon_{i}]=0$ for all $i = 1, \dots, n$.
- **Assumption 3: homoskedasticity.** The covariance matrix of the disturbances $E[\epsilon\epsilon^{\prime}]$ exists, and all its diagonal elements are equal to $\sigma^2$, meaning $E[\epsilon_{i}^2]=\sigma^2$.
- **Assumption 4: no correlation.** The off-diagonal elements of the covariance matrix of the disturbances $E[\epsilon\epsilon^{\prime}]$ are all equal to zero, meaning $E[\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}]=0$ for all $i \ne j$.
- **Assumption 5: constant parameters.** The elements of the $k \times 1$ vector $\beta$ and the scalar $\sigma$ are fixed unknown numbers with $\sigma>0$.
- **Assumption 6: linear model.** The data on the explained variable $y$ have been generated by the data generating process (DGP) $y=X\beta+\epsilon$.
- **Assumption 7: normality.** The disturbances are jointly normally distributed.
    
Under Assumptions 1-7, the values of $y_{i}$ are normally and independently distributed with a constant variance of $\sigma^2$, and varying means $x_{i}\beta$.

### Properties of OLS

**Unbiasedness of $b$**: The expected value of the least squares estimator $b$ is equal to the true parameter vector $\beta$, $E[b]=\beta$.

- Proof (relies on assumptions 1, 2, 5, and 6): substitute $y = X\beta + \epsilon$ into the least squares estimator formulae $b = (X'X)^{-1}X'y$, we get $b=(X^{\prime}X)^{-1}X^{\prime}(X\beta+\epsilon)=\beta+(X^{\prime}X)^{-1}X^{\prime}\epsilon$. The expectation is linear, so we get $E[b]=E[\beta+(X'X)^{-1}X'\epsilon]=\beta+(X'X)^{-1}X'E[\epsilon]$. Since $E[\epsilon]=0$, we get $E[b]=\beta$.

**Covariance Matrix of $b$**: The covariance matrix evaluates the accuracy of the estimators. The diagonal elements represent the variances of the individual parameter estimators, while the off-diagonal elements represent the covariances between these estimators. $Var(b)=\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}$

- Proof (relies on assumptions 1-6): Given that $b$ is an unbiased estimator, the covariance matrix is $Var(b)=E[(b-\beta)(b-\beta)']$. Substituting $(b-\beta)=(X'X)^{-1}X'\epsilon$, and isolating the expectation of the disturbances $E[\epsilon\epsilon']$, and substituting $E[\epsilon\epsilon']=\sigma^2I$, this simplifies to $\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}$.

**$b$ is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE)**: Often referred to as the Guass-Markov theorem, this property states that among all possible linear unbiased estimators, the least squares estimator $b$ has the smallest possible variance.

- Proof (relies on assumptions 1-6): We say that an estimator $b$ is more efficient (has a smaller variance) than another estimator $\tilde{b}$ if the difference between their covariance matrices, $Var(\tilde{b}) - Var(b)$, is a positive semi-definite (p.s.d.) matrix. To prove OLS is BLUE, you define any alternative linear unbiased estimator as $\tilde{b} = ((X'X)^{-1}X' + D)y$. The condition of unbiasedness requires $DX=0$. Calculating the covariance matrix of this alternative estimator, and substituting the requirement results in $Var(\tilde{b}) = \sigma^2(X'X)^{-1} + \sigma^2DD'$. Because $\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}$ is the exact variance of the OLS estimator $b$, and a matrix multiplied by its transpose ($DD'$) is always positive semi-definite, it guarantees that $Var(\tilde{b}) \ge Var(b)$, proving that no other linear unbiased estimator can have a smaller variance.

**Unbiased estimator for the Disturbance Variance** ($s^2$): The true variance of the disturbances, $\sigma^2$, is unknown and must be estimated from the data. The unbiased estimator $s^2$ is based on the sum of squared residuals ($SSR$ or $e'e$) adjusted for the degrees of freedom, defined as $s^2 = \frac{e'e}{n-k}$.

- Proof (relies on assumptions 1-6): Define the symmetric and idempotent residual maker matrix $M = I - X(X'X)^{-1}X'$. We can express the residuals as $e = My = M(X\beta + \epsilon)$. Since $MX=0$, this simplifies to $e = M\epsilon$. The sum of squared residuals is then $e'e = \epsilon'M'M\epsilon = \epsilon'M\epsilon$. Because $\epsilon'M\epsilon$ is a scalar, we can use the trace operator's cyclic property to write $E[e'e] = E[tr(M\epsilon\epsilon')] = tr(ME[\epsilon\epsilon'])$. Substituting $E[\epsilon\epsilon'] = \sigma^2I$, we get $\sigma^2tr(M)$. The trace of $M$ resolves to $n-k$. Thus, $E[e'e] = \sigma^2(n-k)$, which proves that the expectation of $s^2 = \frac{e'e}{n-k}$ equals $\sigma^2$.

### Heteroskedasticity and Auto-correlation

Definitions

- We define the covariance matrix of the error terms $\Omega=E[\epsilon\epsilon']$.
- When both assumption 3 (homoskedasticity), and assumption 4 (no correlation) hold true, then, $\Omega=\sigma^2I$, for constant $\sigma^2$.
- **Heteroskedasticity** means assumption 3 (homoskedasticity) is not satisfied. This indicates that the amount of randomness is not constant, but instead differs for each observation, expressed as $E[\epsilon_{i}^2]=\sigma^2_{i}$. This means $\Omega$ has varying non constant values on its main diagonal.
- **Autocorrelation** (also known as serial correlation) means assumption 4 (no correlation) is not satisfied. This means that the disturbance terms across different observations are correlated with one another, expressed as $E[\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}]=\sigma_{ij}$ for observations where $i\neq j$. This means non-zero values populate the off-diagonal elements of the covariance matrix $\Omega$.
	- Causes for autocorrelation include omitted explanatory variables that are themselves correlated (such as omitting $y_{i-1}$ or $X_{i-1}$). Alternatively, the errors may be inherently autocorrelated, for example, following an Auto Regressive (AR) process of order 1 where $\epsilon_{i}=\rho\epsilon_{i-1}+\eta_{i}$.
- Heteroskedasticity does not imply autocorrelation or the other way around.
- In the presence of heteroskedasticity and/or autocorrelation, OLS is still unbiased and consistent, but it is no longer efficient (not BLUE).

White Standard Errors

- **The True Variance**: Under *heteroskedasticity*, the true variance of the OLS estimator is expressed as $Var(b_{OLS})=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{\prime})^{-1}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}x_{i}x_{i}^{\prime})(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{\prime})^{-1}$.
- **The Estimator**: Because the actual individual variances $\sigma_{i}^{2}$ are unknown, White's approach uses the squared OLS residuals $e_{i}^{2}$ (where $e_{i}=y_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime}b_{OLS}$) as a direct estimator for them. This results in the consistent White estimator for the variance:
	$$
	\hat{Var}(b_{OLS})=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{\prime})^{-1}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{2}x_{i}x_{i}^{\prime})(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{\prime})^{-1}
	$$
- Pros: the specific functional form of the heteroskedasticity does not need to be explicitly modelled. 
- Cons: while the standard errors are corrected, the OLS estimator itself remains inefficient and is not BLUE.

Newey-West estimator

- **The True Variance**: Under *heteroskedasticity and/or autocorrelation*, the true variance of the OLS estimator is expressed as: 
	$$
	Var(b_{OLS})=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}X^{\prime}X\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}X^{\prime}\Omega X\left(\frac{1}{n}X^{\prime}X\right)^{-1}
	$$
	Only in the absence of correlation and with homoskedasticity ($\Omega=\sigma^{2}I$) does this simplify to the usual $\sigma^{2}(X^{\prime}X)^{-1}$. Under heteroskedasticity and/or autocorrelation, $\Omega$ cannot be simplified in this way.
- **The Estimator**: The Newey-West estimator produces Heteroskedasticity and Autocorrelation Consistent (HAC) standard errors. It estimates the variance by applying a kernel (such as the Bartlett kernel), denoted as $w_{h}$, to weight the cross-products of the residuals based on their distance in time. Defined as:
	$$
	\hat{Var}(b_{OLS})=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}X^{\prime}X\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}X^{\prime}\Omega X\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}X^{\prime}X\right)^{-1}
	$$
	where the term $\frac{1}{n}X^{\prime}\Omega X$ is estimated by:
	$$
	\frac{1}{n}X^{\prime}\Omega X=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{2}x_{i}x_{i}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}w_{|j-i|}e_{i}e_{j}(x_{i}x_{j}^{\prime}+x_{j}x_{i}^{\prime})
	$$

Generalized Least Squares

- **Generalized Least Squares** (**GLS**) is the preferred estimation solution when the exact form of the covariance matrix $\Omega$ is known. The idea is to transform the data ($X$ and $y$) such that standard conditions for OLS hold true, and it becomes BLUE again. 
	- Because $\Omega$ is symmetric and positive definite, it can be decomposed to find a lower triangular matrix $P$ such that $PP'=\Omega$. 
	- This transforms the disturbances $\epsilon^*=P^{-1}\epsilon$ such that they are homoskedastic and completely free of autocorrelation. 
	- The original data is transformed aswell matrix, defining the new variables as $y^*=P^{-1}y$, and $X^*=P^{-1}X$. 
	- Applying the OLS method to this newly transformed model yields the GLS estimator calculated as $b_{GLS}=(X'\Omega^{-1} X)^{-1}X'\Omega^{-1}y$.
	- Because applying OLS to the transformed model fulfills all standard assumptions, the GLS estimator is BLUE.
- **Weighted Least Squares** (**WLS**) is a simplified case of GLS that is applied when the model is heteroskedastic, but not autocorrelated. Here we weigh the error of each observation by $w_{i}^2=\frac{1}{v_{i}}$ where $v_{i}$ is a known proportional variance factor (such that $\sigma_{i}^2=\sigma^2 v_{i}$). This acts as a weighting mechanism where observations with larger variance are given less weight in the estimation, ensuring OLS on the weighted observations is the BLUE.

Feasible Generalized Least Squares

- In practical applications the true structure of $\Omega$ is usually unknown. We we need a model to determine them.
- **Feasible Generalized Least Squares** (**FGLS**) solves this by estimating the $\Omega$ matrix before applying the GLS transformations. 
	- We apply standard OLS to obtain consistent parameter estimates. 
	- We determine residuals from this step, and use it to estimate the unknown covariance matrix $\hat{\Omega}$, which is used to construct the transformation matrix $\hat{P}$. 
	- Finally OLS is applied to the data transformed by $\hat{P}^{-1}$ to calculate the $b_{FGLS}$ estimator. 
	- This process may be repeated until the estimation of the parameters stops changing (which is called iterated FGLS).
	- Note: because the true $\Omega$ matrix contains too many unknown parameters to freely estimate from out limited sample of residuals, we must assume it follows a specific structure or model. By defining a model for the variances, we can estimate the entire matrix using just a few parameters.
- **Feasible weighted least squares** (**FWLS**) is a simplified case of FGLS that is applied when the model is heteroskedastic, but not autocorrelated.
- **Iterative Cochrane-Orcutt Procedure**: This procedure is used when the errors are themselves autocorrelated, for example, following an Auto Regressive (AR) model of order 1. 
	- The model is defined as $y_{i}=\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}x_{i}+\epsilon_{i}$, where the errors follow $\epsilon_{i}=\rho\epsilon_{i-1}+\eta_{i}$, and standard assumptions apply to $\eta_{i}$. 
	- This allows the model to be transformed into $y_{i}-\rho y_{i-1}=\beta_{1}(1-\rho)+\beta_{2}(x_{i}-\rho x_{i-1})+\eta_{i}$. 
	- Given a specific value for $\rho$, the parameters $\beta_{1}$ and $\beta_{2}$ can be estimated using OLS. Conversely, given estimates for $\beta$, the parameter $\rho$ can be estimated using OLS. 
	- The process of alternating between these estimations is known as the iterative Cochrane-Orcutt procedure.

Tests for heteroskedasticity

- $H_{0}$: Homoskedasticity, and $H_{1}$: Heteroskedasticity
- **Goldfeld-Quandt**: Tests for heteroskedasticity without knowledge about what is causing it.
	- Split the data in 2 groups of $n_{1}$, and $n_{2}$ observations. These groups are made based on the suspicion of heteroskedasticity.
	- Apply OLS on all groups to derive their respective residuals $e_{i}$.
	- Compare estimated variances of the disturbances $s^2_{i}=\frac{e_{i}'e_{i}}{n-k}$. Where $s_{2}^2$ is the group with larger disturbances.
	- Under $H_{0}$:
		$$
		GQ = \left( \frac{s_{2}}{s_{1}} \right)^2 \sim F(n_{2}-k, n_{1}-k)
		$$
- **White and Breusch-Pagan LM Tests**: Both the White and Breusch-Pagan tests are Lagrange Multiplier (LM) tests. The core intuition behind both is to determine if the variance of the residuals can be explained by a set of independent variables. Since we do not observe the true variances ($\sigma_i^2$), we use the squared OLS residuals ($e_i^2$) as a proxy. If an auxiliary regression of these squared residuals on a set of additional explanatory variables $z_{i}$ yields a high $R^2$, it means the variance is systematically related to those variables, indicating heteroskedasticity.
	- General Procedure:
		- Run standard OLS on the original model ($y_i = x_i^\prime\beta + \epsilon_i$) to obtain the residuals $e_i$
		- Run an auxiliary regression using the squared residuals as the dependent variable: $e_i^2 = z_i^\prime\gamma + \eta_i$. (The choice of $z_i$ is where the two tests differ). 
		- Calculate the test statistic using the sample size $n$ and the $R^2$ of the auxiliary regression: $LM = n \times R^2$.
		- Under $H_0$, this test statistic follows a Chi-square distribution, $\chi^2(p-1)$, where $p$ is the total number of parameters estimated in the auxiliary regression (including the intercept which is left out, hence the $-1$).
	- **Breusch-Pagan Test**: This test is used when you have a specific theory or suspicion about *which* variables are driving the variance. You explicitly define the $z_i$ variables in the auxiliary regression based on this suspicion.
	- **White Test**: The White test is much more agnostic and serves as a general "catch-all" test for heteroskedasticity. You do not need external variables. Instead, it tests the null hypothesis that $\gamma_2 = \dots = \gamma_p = 0$ using the original explanatory variables. There are two variations of the auxiliary regression:
		- *White without cross-terms*: Regress $e_i^2$ on a constant (intercept), all original regressors, and their squared values.
		- *White with cross-terms*: Regress $e_i^2$ on a constant, all original regressors, their squared values, and all unique cross-products (interactions) between the original regressors.
- **Likelihood Ratio Test**: test evaluates the presence of heteroskedasticity by estimating your model twice using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) and comparing the fit of those two models.
	- The Restricted Model ($H_0$) operates under the strict assumption of homoskedasticity. It forces the variance of all error terms to be a single, constant value ($\sigma^2$) across all observations. Estimating this model yields the restricted log-likelihood value, $\log L_r$.
	- The Unrestricted Model ($H_1$) allows the variance to differ across observations (heteroskedasticity). It estimates the same base regression but simultaneously models the variance as a function of specific observable variables, typically written as $\sigma_i^2 = h(z_i^\prime\gamma)$. Estimating this model yields the unrestricted log-likelihood value, $\log L_u$.
	- Because the unrestricted model is "free" to allow variances to change, it will mathematically always fit the data at least as well as the restricted model. The LR test evaluates exactly how much *worse* the model's fit gets when you force the restriction of homoskedasticity upon it.
		$$
		LR=-2(\log L_r - \log L_u) \sim \chi^2(k)
		$$
	- The parameter $k$ is equal to the number of restrictions imposed on the restricted model. In practice, this is the number of $\gamma$ parameters (excluding the constant) in your variance function $h(z_i^\prime\gamma)$ that you forced to be zero in the restricted model.
	- *Note*: Calculating a likelihood inherently means calculating the probability of observing your specific data. To calculate these probabilities and obtain $\log L_r$ and $\log L_u$, you must make a strict mathematical assumption about the shape of the error disturbances. In the standard linear model, this requires the assumption that the errors are normally distributed, $\epsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2I)$. Without assuming normality, the likelihood function cannot be constructed, and the LR test cannot be performed.

Tests for autocorrelation

- All four of the following tests assess the null hypothesis of no autocorrelation. They make (indirect) use of the autocorrelation of the OLS residuals, which is defined as 
	$$
	r_{k}=\frac{\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}e_{i}e_{i-k}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{2}}
	$$
- **Durbin-Watson (DW) Test**
    - *Intuition*: The test evaluates the relationship between successive residuals to detect first-order autocorrelation.
    - *Test Statistic*: The statistic is calculated as the sum of squared differences between successive residuals divided by the total sum of squared residuals. The formula is $DW=\frac{\sum_{i=2}^{n}(e_{i}-e_{i-1})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{2}}$, which can be approximated as $2(1-r_{1})$.
    - *Distribution*: The value of the DW statistic ranges between 0 (representing perfect positive correlation, where $r_{1}=1$) and 4 (representing perfect negative correlation, where $r_{1}=-1$). Under the null hypothesis of no autocorrelation, the value should be approximately 2. The exact distribution under the null depends on the properties of the included regressors.
    - *Limitations*: This test is not applicable when lagged dependent variables are included as regressors.
- **Box-Pierce (BP) Test**
    - *Intuition*: This test evaluates whether the squared autocorrelations of the residuals up to a specified lag $p$ are jointly zero.
    - *Test Statistic*: The statistic is the sample size $n$ multiplied by the sum of the squared autocorrelations up to lag $p$. The mathematical formulation is $BP=n\sum_{k=1}^{p}r_{k}^{2}$.
    - *Distribution*: Under the null hypothesis of no autocorrelation, the BP statistic asymptotically follows a Chi-square distribution with $p$ degrees of freedom, denoted as $\chi^{2}(p)$.
    - *Limitations*: This test is only applicable when the regressors are nonstochastic, meaning it cannot natively be used if lagged dependent variables are included, though corrections are possible.
- **Ljung-Box (LB) Test**
    - *Intuition*: Similar to the Box-Pierce test, this evaluates the joint significance of autocorrelations up to lag $p$, but it applies a specific weighting to improve finite-sample performance.
    - *Test Statistic*: The formula introduces a time-based weight to the squared autocorrelations. It is calculated as $LB=n\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{n+2}{n-k}r_{k}^{2}$.
    - *Distribution*: Under the null hypothesis of no autocorrelation, the LB statistic also follows a Chi-square distribution with $p$ degrees of freedom, $\chi^{2}(p)$.
    - *Limitations*: Like the Box-Pierce test, the LB test is restricted to models with nonstochastic regressors.
- **Breusch-Godfrey (BG) Test**
    - *Intuition*: This is a Lagrange Multiplier (LM) test that evaluates the restriction that all autoregressive parameters $\gamma$ up to lag $p$ are equal to zero ($H_{0}:\gamma_{1}=...=\gamma_{p}=0$) in the error model $\epsilon_{i}=\gamma_{1}\epsilon_{i-1}+...+\gamma_{p}\epsilon_{i-p}+\eta_{i}$. Because it accommodates different model structures, it is the most generally applicable test for autocorrelation.
    - *Test Statistic*: First, apply standard OLS to the original model ($y_{i}=x_{i}^{\prime}\beta+\eta_{i}$) to retrieve the residuals $e_{i}$. Next, run an auxiliary regression of those residuals against their lagged values and the original regressors ($e_{i}=\gamma_{1}e_{i-1}+...+\gamma_{p}e_{i-p}+x_{i}^{\prime}\delta+\omega_{i}$). The test statistic is calculated by multiplying the sample size $n$ by the $R^{2}$ of this auxiliary regression ($LM=nR^{2}$).
    - *Distribution*: Under the null hypothesis of no autocorrelation, the test statistic follows a Chi-square distribution, $\chi^{2}(p)$, where the parameter $p$ represents the number of lags included for the residuals.

### Endogeneity

Definitions

- We replace Assumption $1$ (fixed regressors) with Assumption $1^{*}$, which treats the regressors $X_j$ as stochastic variables, such that $plim (\frac{1}{n}X^{\prime}X)=Q_{XX}$.
- Regressors $X$ are considered **exogenous** when the orthogonality condition holds, meaning $plim(\frac{1}{n}X^{\prime}\epsilon)=0$.
- Regressors $X$ are considered **endogenous** when this condition fails, meaning $plim(\frac{1}{n}X^{\prime}\epsilon)\ne0$, which implies that the covariance between at least one regressor and the error term is not zero ($cov(x_{ji},\epsilon_{i})\ne0$).
- **Causes of Endogeneity**: There are three important causes for endogeneity:
	- Omitted variables: Failing to include a relevant variable.
	- Measurement errors: Inaccuracies in data collection, such as non-compliance in natural experiments.
	- Simultaneity: When variables influence each other concurrently, such as the interaction between inflation and central bank interest rates.
- If even a single explanatory variable is endogenous, the OLS estimator becomes biased and inconsistent because $plim(b) \ne \beta$. Because of this inconsistency, standard asymptotic tests are no longer applicable. If no variables are endogenous, the orthogonality condition is met, and OLS remains consistent.

Circumventing Endogeneity

- **Instrumental Variables (IV)**: To solve endogeneity, we look for instrumental variables ($Z_i$) that do not directly affect the outcome $y_i$ but are correlated with the explanatory variables $X_j$. For these instruments to be valid, they must fulfill four criteria:
	1. *Uncorrelated with the error*: The instruments must be exogenous, meaning $plim\frac{1}{n}Z^{\prime}\epsilon=0$.
	2. *Correlated with endogenous variables*: They must relate to the regressors, such that $plim\frac{1}{n}Z^{\prime}X=Q_{ZX}$, satisfying the rank condition $rank(Q_{ZX})=k$.
	3. *Order condition*: There must be at least as many instruments ($m$) as there are endogenous regressors ($k$), meaning $m \ge k$.
	4. *Stable and not multicollinear*: The instruments must satisfy $plim\frac{1}{n}Z^{\prime}Z=Q_{ZZ}$ with a rank equal to $m$.
- The Core Idea: The underlying strategy is to explain the endogenous variable $X$ using the instrument $Z$. By definition, the part of $X$ that is explained by $Z$ is not correlated with the error term $\epsilon$. We then use this "clean," explained part to estimate $y$.
- Instrumental Variable (IV) estimation and 2-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) are treated as the same procedure. The 2SLS method involves two distinct steps to estimate the model $y=X\beta+\epsilon$.
	- Stage 1: Regress the explanatory variables $X$ on the instruments $Z$ to obtain the predicted values $\hat{X}$. It is crucial that all exogenous regressors are included in this step. Here $\hat{X}=Z(Z^{\prime}Z)^{-1}Z^{\prime}X=P_{Z}X$. 
	- Stage 2: Regress the dependent variable $y$ on these predicted values $\hat{X}$. Which yields This second stage yields the estimator formula $b_{IV}=(\hat{X}^{\prime}\hat{X})^{-1}\hat{X}^{\prime}y$. The estimator can be rewritten and expanded as $b_{IV}=(X^{\prime}P_{Z}^{\prime}P_{Z}X)^{-1}X^{\prime}P_{Z}^{\prime}y$, which simplifies to $b_{IV}=(X^{\prime}P_{Z}X)^{-1}X^{\prime}P_{Z}y$.
	- A model is "exactly identified" when the number of instruments ($m$) exactly equals the number of explanatory variables ($k$). In this case, the estimator simplifies to the standard IV formula: $b_{IV}=(Z^{\prime}X)^{-1}Z^{\prime}y$.
- The IV/2SLS estimator, denoted as $b_{IV}$ or $b_{2SLS}$, is consistent, and asymptotically normal if the instruments are strongly correlated with the explanatory variables, specifically: $\sqrt{n}(b_{IV}-\beta) \sim N(0,\sigma^{2}(Q_{ZX}^{\prime}Q_{ZZ}^{-1}Q_{ZX})^{-1})$. 

Tests

- **Hausman LM-test**
    - *Idea*: This test is used to evaluate whether the explanatory variables are exogenous. It operates under the null hypothesis ($H_0$) that the suspected endogenous regressor is actually exogenous.
    - *Computation*: The LM statistic is calculated using the following steps:
        - Step 1a: Regress $y$ on the exogenous variables ($X^{exo}$) and the endogenous variables ($x^{endo}$) to obtain the residuals $e_i = y_i - b_1x_i^{exo} - b_2x_i^{endo}$.
        - Step 1b: Regress the endogenous variables $x^{endo}$ on the instruments $Z$ to calculate the residuals $\hat{v}_i$.
        - Step 2: Regress the first-step residuals $e$ on $X^{exo}$, $x^{endo}$, and the residuals $\hat{v}_i$.
        - Step 3: Compute the test statistic by multiplying the sample size $n$ by the $R^2$ of this final regression, $LM = nR^2$. 
    - *Distribution*: Under $H_0$, the test statistic approximately follows a Chi-square distribution, denoted as $\chi^2(k_0)$.
    - *Derivation of Parameters*: The degrees of freedom parameter $k_0$ represents the number of variables that are potentially endogenous.
- **Sargan Test**
    - *Idea*: This test evaluates whether the chosen instruments are valid. It evaluates the null hypothesis ($H_0$) that the instruments are valid.
    - *Computation*: The statistic is calculated using a 3-step LM approach:
        - Step 1: Apply IV estimation on the model $y = X\beta + \epsilon$ using instruments $Z$ to obtain the IV residuals $e_{IV} = y - Xb_{IV}$.
        - Step 2: Regress the IV residuals $e_{IV}$ on the instruments $Z$, which involves performing an OLS regression on the model $e_{IV,i} = z_i^{\prime}\gamma + \eta_i$.
        - Step 3: Compute the test statistic as the sample size multiplied by the $R^2$ of this regression, $LM = nR^2$.
    - *Distribution*: Under $H_0$, the test statistic approximately follows a Chi-square distribution, denoted as $\chi^2(m-k)$.
    - *Derivation of Parameters*: The degrees of freedom are derived as $m - k$, where $m$ is the total number of instruments and $k$ is the number of explanatory variables.
    - *Conditions*: This procedure only works if there are more instruments than strictly necessary. The model must be overidentified, meaning $m > k$.
- **Tests for Weak Instruments**
    - *Idea*: These tests evaluate whether the instruments are strong enough. The tests rely on the concentration parameter, which measures the strength of the instruments.
    - *Computation & Distribution*: The specific test used depends on the number of endogenous variables.
        - For $k = 1$: The test relies on the first stage regression F-statistic to evaluate the hypothesis that $\gamma = 0$.
        - For $k > 1$: The Cragg-Donald (1993) test is applied, which acts as a multivariate version of the F-test.
    - *Conditions and Consequences*:
        - A general benchmark is that if the F-statistic is less than 10 ($F < 10$), the instruments are weak.
        - If the instruments are strong (a large concentration parameter), the IV estimator follows a normal distribution.
        - If the instruments are weak, the IV estimator has a non-standard distribution.
        - When dealing with weak instruments, standard inference is compromised, and one should instead rely on Anderson-Rubin, KLM, or CLR statistics.
