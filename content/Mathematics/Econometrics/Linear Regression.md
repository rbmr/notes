### Foundations

The **Data Generating Process** (**DGP**) is the combined economic and measurement process that yields the observed economic data. For example, a DGP might involve the purchase decisions of many individual buyers across multiple stores, combined with how those sales are recorded.

An **econometric model** is a concise and reasonably accurate reflection of the data generating process. By disregarding less relevant aspects of the data, the model helps us better understand the DGP's main features.

The **true model** is an idealized concept reflecting a situation where an econometric model perfectly describes the DGP. Because an econometric model is always an approximation, a literal "true model" does not make much practical sense. However, it is a helpful mathematical fiction used to analyze the statistical properties of estimation methods.

### Linear Model

The true **linear model** postulates that a dependent variable $y$ depends linearly on unknown parameters $\beta$, and explanatory variables $X$, plus some unobserved disturbance term $\epsilon$. This model is expressed in matrix form as:

$$
y = X\beta+\epsilon
$$

In practice we obtain a finite sample of data that has possible been generated by this process. From this process we actually observe:
- $y$: an $n \times 1$ vector of observations for the dependent variable.
- $X$ an $n \times k$ matrix of observations of the explanatory variables.

We do not know the underlying parameters of the true model. Both the $k \times 1$ parameter vector $\beta$, and the $n \times 1$ vector of random disturbances $\epsilon$ remain completely unknown.

The primary task in econometric analysis is to estimate these parameters as accurately as possible using our observed data $X$ and $y$. If $b$ is a $k \times 1$ vector of estimates for the true parameters $\beta$, the estimated model is written as:

$$
y = Xb+e
$$

Here, $e$ denotes the $n \times 1$ vector of **residuals**, representing the observable estimation errors, calculated as the difference between our actual data and the fitted values $e = y - Xb$. Furthermore, the predicted values may be denoted as $\hat{y}$ (pronounced "y-hat"), and calculated as $\hat{y}=Xb$. Then the residuals are simply $e = y - \hat{y}$.

### Loss Functions

When fitting a model to data, a criterion (or loss) function evaluates the fit by penalizing deviations.

- **Quadratic Loss (Least Squares)** is the most frequently used criterion, measuring the sum of squared deviations, $S(b)=\sum_{i}e_{i}^2 =e'e=(y-Xb)'(y-Xb)$. This method is commonly referred to as **ordinary least squares** (**OLS**).
- **Absolute Loss** is an alternative function that relies on the absolute values of the deviations, $S_{abs}(b)=\sum_{i}|e_{i}|$.
- **Huber's Loss** combines the best properties of both quadratic and absolute loss. It is quadratic for small errors (where $|r_{i}| \le \delta$) making it differentiable at zero, and linear for large errors (where $|r_{i}| > \delta$), making it more robust to outliers, $S_{h}(b)=\sum_{i}L_{\delta}(e_{i})$
    $$
    L_{\delta}(a) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2}(a)^2 & \text{if } |a| \leq \delta \\ \delta \left(|a| - \frac{\delta}{2}\right) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
    $$

From here on, we will only be concerned with minimizing the quadratic loss. 

### Coefficient of Determination $R^2$

To find the vector of estimates $b$ that minimizes the quadratic loss we set the first-order derivatives of the loss function $S(b)$ to zero, and solve for $b$. This results in the least square estimator $b = (X'X)^{-1}X'y$.

The performance of the least squares method can be evaluated by decomposing the total sample variation.
- **Total Sum of Squares** (**SST**): the total variation in the dependent variable $y$, calculated as $SST = \sum_{i}(y_{i}-\overline{y})^2$. 
- **Explained Sum of Squares** (**SSE**): the portion of the variation that is explained by the model. $SSE=\sum_{i}(\hat{y}_{i}-\overline{y})^2$
- **Sum of Squared Residuals** (**SSR**): the unexplained, residual part of the variation, calculated as $SSR = \sum_{i} e_{i}^2 = e'e$.
- Relation: $SST = SSE + SSR$.

The **coefficient of determination**, denoted by $R^2$ (R-squared), measures the fraction of the total sample variation that is explained by the model. It is defined as the relative explained sum of squares: $R^2=\frac{SSE}{SST}=1-\frac{SSR}{SST}$. Minimizing the least squares criterion is equivalent to maximizing $R^2$. If the model contains a constant (intercept) term, then $0 \leq R^2 \leq 1$. If not, then $SSR$ may be larger than $SST$, making $R^2 < 0$.

When explanatory variables are added to a model, $R^2$ value never decreases. However, this does not necessarily mean the model is improving in a meaningful way. This motivates the **adjusted** $R^2$ ($\bar{R}^2$) to adjust for the degrees of freedom $k$ (the number of explanatory variables + 1 for the intercept) using the following formula:
$$
\overline{R}^2 =  1-\frac{n-1}{n-k}(1-R^2)
$$

### Seven Assumptions

The rather strict conditions outlined in the seven assumptions are introduced to simplify the analytical proofs and allow for mathematically exact results when determining the statistical properties and accuracy of least squares estimation.

- **Assumption 1: fixed regressors.** All elements of the $n \times k$ matrix $X$ containing the observations on the explanatory variables are non-stochastic. It is assumed that $n \ge k$ and that the matrix $X$ has rank $k$.
- **Assumption 2: random disturbances, zero mean.** The $n \times 1$ vector $\epsilon$ consists of random disturbances with a mean of zero, meaning $E[\epsilon_{i}]=0$ for all $i = 1, \dots, n$.
- **Assumption 3: homoskedasticity.** The covariance matrix of the disturbances $E[\epsilon\epsilon^{\prime}]$ exists, and all its diagonal elements are equal to $\sigma^2$, meaning $E[\epsilon_{i}^2]=\sigma^2$.
- **Assumption 4: no correlation.** The off-diagonal elements of the covariance matrix of the disturbances $E[\epsilon\epsilon^{\prime}]$ are all equal to zero, meaning $E[\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}]=0$ for all $i \ne j$.
- **Assumption 5: constant parameters.** The elements of the $k \times 1$ vector $\beta$ and the scalar $\sigma$ are fixed unknown numbers with $\sigma>0$.
- **Assumption 6: linear model.** The data on the explained variable $y$ have been generated by the data generating process (DGP) $y=X\beta+\epsilon$.
- **Assumption 7: normality.** The disturbances are jointly normally distributed.
    
Under Assumptions 1-7, the values of $y_{i}$ are normally and independently distributed with a constant variance of $\sigma^2$, and varying means $x_{i}\beta$.

### Properties

**Unbiasedness**: The expected value of the least squares estimator $b$ is equal to the true parameter vector $\beta$, $E[b]=\beta$.

- Proof (relies on assumptions 1, 2, 5, and 6): substitute $y = X\beta + \epsilon$ into the least squares estimator formulae $b = (X'X)^{-1}X'y$, we get $b=(X^{\prime}X)^{-1}X^{\prime}(X\beta+\epsilon)=\beta+(X^{\prime}X)^{-1}X^{\prime}\epsilon$. The expectation is linear, so we get $E[b]=E[\beta+(X'X)^{-1}X'\epsilon]=\beta+(X'X)^{-1}X'E[\epsilon]$. Since $E[\epsilon]=0$, we get $E[b]=\beta$.

**Covariance Matrix of $b$**: The covariance matrix evaluates the accuracy of the estimators. The diagonal elements represent the variances of the individual parameter estimators, while the off-diagonal elements represent the covariances between these estimators. $Var(b)=\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}$

- Proof (relies on assumptions 1-6): Given that $b$ is an unbiased estimator, the covariance matrix is $Var(b)=E[(b-\beta)(b-\beta)']$.
