### Sequences

- A **sequence** is an ordered list of numbers $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots$, i.e. a function where the domain is the set of all natural numbers.
	- Notation: $\{a_n\}$ or $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$, where $a_n$ is the **term** at index $n$.
- **Definition of the limit of a sequence**: $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L$ if for every $\epsilon > 0$ there exists an $N > 0$ such that if $n > N$, then $|a_n - L| < \epsilon$. 
	- This is the limit-to-infinity definition from [[1. Univariate Calculus]], restricted to integer inputs.
- A sequence is **convergent** if its limit exists (and is finite), and **divergent** otherwise.
- Relation to limits of functions: if $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L$ and $a_n = f(n)$, then $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L$. 
	- Consequently, the calculation rules for limits (sums, products, quotients, the squeeze theorem, growth rate comparisons) from [[1. Univariate Calculus]] carry over to sequences unchanged.
	- Note the converse does not hold, for example $a_n = \sin(2\pi n) = 0$ converges, while $\sin(2\pi x)$ has no limit.
- **Monotone Convergence Theorem**: every bounded monotone sequence converges. Specifically, an increasing sequence that is bounded above converges (to the supremum of its terms), and a decreasing sequence that is bounded below converges (to the infimum).

### Recursive Sequences

- A sequence can also be defined **recursively**. A recursive definition consists of one or more initial terms (the **base cases**) together with an **update rule** that produces each term from the preceding ones.
	- A **fixed point** of a function $g$ is a point $p$ such that $g(p) = p$, i.e. applying the update rule leaves it unchanged.
- **Theorem (limits are fixed points)**: if a recursive sequence $a_{n+1} = g(a_n)$ converges to $L$ and $g$ is continuous at $L$, then $L$ is a fixed point of $g$. Proof: $g(L) = g(\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} g(a_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} a_{n+1} = L$.
	- In practice we use the monotone convergence theorem to show a recursive sequence converges, after which the fixed-point theorem identifies the limit.
- **Theorem (convergence of simple iteration)**: let $g: [a,b] \to [a,b]$ (a function that maps an interval onto itself) be differentiable with $|g'(x)| \leq k$ for all $x \in [a,b]$, for some constant $k < 1$. Then:
	1. $g$ has exactly one fixed point $p$ in $[a,b]$
	2. for any starting point $a_1 \in [a,b]$, the recursive sequence $a_{n+1} = g(a_n)$ converges to $p$, with $|a_n - p| \leq k^{n-1}|a_1 - p|$.
	- Intuition: by the Mean Value Theorem, $|g(x) - g(y)| \leq k|x-y|$, so every application of $g$ shrinks the distance to the fixed point by at least a factor $k$ (a *contraction*). The error bound is that of a geometric sequence (see below).

### Series

- A **series** is the sum of the terms of a sequence. The **$N$-th partial sum** of $\{a_n\}$ is:
	$$
	s_N = \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n
	$$
- An **infinite series** is the sum of an infinite sequence, defined as the limit of its partial sum:
	$$
	s_{\infty}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n = \lim_{N \to \infty} s_N
	$$
- The series **converges** if this limit exists (and is finite), and **diverges** otherwise. Convergence of a series is nothing more than convergence of the sequence of partial sums $\{s_N\}$.
- Calculation rules: if $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ are convergent series and $c$ is a constant, then:
	- $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c\, a_n = c \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$
	- $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (a_n \pm b_n) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n \pm \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n$
- Note: changing, adding, or removing finitely many terms never affects *whether* a series converges (it shifts every partial sum by a constant), though it does affect the value. Convergence is decided entirely by the tail.

### Geometric Series

- A **geometric sequence** is a sequence where all neighboring terms have a **common ratio** $r$. That is, $a_n = a\, r^{n-1}$ for a first term $a \neq 0$.
- A **geometric series** is the series of a geometric sequence: $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a\,r^{n-1} = a + ar + ar^2 + \dots$
- **Theorem (partial sums)**: for $r \neq 1$:
	$$
	s_N = \sum_{n=1}^{N} a\,r^{n-1} = \frac{a(1 - r^N)}{1 - r}
	$$
	- Derivation: subtract $r\,s_N$ from $s_N$, then all terms cancel except the first and the last: $s_N - r s_N = a - a r^N$, rearrange to get the formula above.
	- For $r = 1$, trivially $s_N = Na$.
- **Theorem (infinite geometric series)**: the geometric series converges if and only if $|r| < 1$, in which case:
	$$
	\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a\,r^{n-1} = \frac{a}{1-r}
	$$
	This follows directly from the partial sum formula: $\lim_{ n \to \infty }r^N = 0$ if and only if $|r| < 1$. For $|r| \geq 1$ the terms do not shrink to zero, and the series diverges: the partial sums grow without bound (e.g. $r = 1$) or oscillate indefinitely (e.g. $r = -1$).

### Arithmetic Series

- An **arithmetic sequence** is a sequence with a **common difference** $d$: each term is the previous term plus $d$, i.e. $a_n = a + (n-1)d$ for a first term $a$:
	$$
	a,\ a+d,\ a+2d,\ a+3d,\ \dots
	$$
- The ordered sequence of positive integers $1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$ (i.e. $a_n = n$) are the arithmetic sequence with $a = d = 1$.
- An **arithmetic series** is the series of an arithmetic sequence.
- **Theorem (sum of the first $n$ positive integers)**:
	$$
	1 + 2 + 3 + \dots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}
	$$
	- Derivation (Gauss's trick): write the sum forwards and backwards, and add the two lines term by term:
	$$
	\begin{aligned}
	S_n &= 1 &&+\ 2 &&+ \dots &&+\ n \\
	S_n &= n &&+\ (n-1) &&+ \dots &&+\ 1 \\
	2S_n &= (n+1) &&+\ (n+1) &&+ \dots &&+\ (n+1)
	\end{aligned}
	$$
	There are $n$ columns, each summing to $n+1$, so $2S_n = n(n+1)$, rewriting gives the formula above.

- **Theorem (partial sums)**:
	$$
	s_N = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \big[a + (n-1)d\big] = \frac{N}{2}\big(2a + (N-1)d\big) = \frac{N}{2}(a + a_N)
	$$
	- Derivation: Rearrange the sum to separate out the sum of the first $n$ positive integers: $s_N = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \big[a + (n-1)d\big]=Na+d\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}n$, then apply Gauss' trick to get $s_N = Na + d\,\frac{N(N-1)}{2} = \frac{N}{2}(2a + (N-1)d)$.
	- The last form reads naturally: the number of terms, times the average of the first and last term.
- Trivially, the infinite arithmetic series converges only if both $d=a=0$. Otherwise, the terms never shrink towards zero, so the partial sums keep drifting away.

### Fibonacci Sequence

- The **Fibonacci sequence** is defined as the sequence where each term is the sum of the two preceding terms, so two initial terms are needed to get started.
	- Base cases: $F_0 = 0$ and $F_1 = 1$.
	- Update rule: $F_n = F_{n-1} + F_{n-2}$ for $n \geq 2$.
	- This produces $0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, \dots$
- **Binet's formula** computes $F_n$ directly, without unrolling the recursion:
	$$
	F_n = \frac{\phi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt{5}}, \qquad \text{where } \phi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2},\quad \psi = \frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}
	$$
	(Proof not included.) Since $|\psi| < 1$, the $\psi^n$ term vanishes as $n$ grows: the Fibonacci numbers grow like a geometric sequence with common ratio $\phi \approx 1.618$ (the golden ratio).
- **Theorem (finite Fibonacci series)**:
	$$
	\sum_{k=0}^{n} F_k = F_{n+2} - 1
	$$
	- Derivation (telescoping sum): rewrite the update rule $F_{k}=F_{k-1}+F_{k-2}$  as $F_k = F_{k+2} - F_{k+1}$, and sum it over $k = 0, \dots, n$: 
		$$
		\begin{aligned}
		\sum_{k=0}^{n} F_k &= (F_2 - F_1) + (F_3 - F_2) + \dots + (F_{n+2} - F_{n+1})\\
		\sum_{k=0}^{n} F_k &= F_{n+2} - F_1 = F_{n+2} - 1
	\end{aligned}
		$$
- Trivially, the infinite Fibonacci series diverges: the terms grow rather than shrink to zero.

### Convergence Tests for Series

- **Theorem (necessary condition)**: if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges, then $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0$. Proof not included. 
	- The converse does not hold: the harmonic series $\sum^\infty_{n=1} 1/n$ diverges even though $\lim_{ n \to \infty }1/n= 0$ (see p-series below). 
	- Terms going to zero is necessary, not sufficient.
- **Divergence Test** (the contrapositive): if $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n$ does not exist, or exists but is not $0$, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ diverges.
- **Integral Test**: let $f$ be a continuous, positive, decreasing function on $[1, \infty)$ with $a_n = f(n)$. Then:
	$$
	\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n \text{ converges} \iff \int_1^{\infty} f(x)\,dx \text{ converges}
	$$
	Intuition: the partial sums are Riemann sums of $f$ with width-1 rectangles. Taking left endpoints overestimates the integral and taking right endpoints underestimates it, so the series and the integral trap each other:
	$$
	\int_1^{\infty} f(x)\,dx \;\leq\; \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n \;\leq\; a_1 + \int_1^{\infty} f(x)\,dx
	$$
- **Comparison Test**: suppose $0 \leq a_n \leq b_n$ for all $n$ (or eventually).
	- If the larger series $\sum^{\infty}_{n=1} b_n$ converges, then the smaller series $\sum^\infty_{n=1} a_n$ also converges.
	- If the smaller series $\sum^\infty_{n=1} a_n$ diverges, then the larger series $\sum^{\infty}_{n=1} b_n$ also diverges.
	- Intuition: for non-negative terms, partial sums are increasing, so by the Monotone Convergence Theorem convergence is equivalent to boundedness, and the smaller partial sums are bounded by the larger ones.
- **Limit Comparison Test**: suppose $a_n, b_n > 0$ and
	$$
	\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{a_n}{b_n} = c
	$$
	for a finite constant $c > 0$. Then $\sum^\infty_{n=1} a_n$ and $\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n$ either both converge or both diverge. Use this when the terms are asymptotically proportional but a direct term-wise inequality is awkward to establish.

### p-series

- A **p-sequence** is a sequence whose terms are a constant $c$ divided by the index raised to a fixed power $p > 0$: $a_n = c/n^p$. 
- The corresponding series is called a **p-series** (also known as a *hyperharmonic series*):
	$$
	\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{c}{n^p} = c\left(\frac{1}{1^p} + \frac{1}{2^p} + \frac{1}{3^p} + \dots\right)
	$$
	- Since the constant factors out it does not affect convergence. For easier writing, we take $c = 1$ below. 
	- The case $p = 1$ is famously known as the **harmonic series** $\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n}$.
- **Theorem**: the p-series converges if and only if $p > 1$. In particular, the harmonic series diverges.
	- Derivation via the Integral Test with $f(x) = 1/x^p$ (continuous, positive, and decreasing on $[1,\infty)$):
		- For $p \neq 1$: $\int_1^{\infty} x^{-p}\,dx = \left[\frac{x^{1-p}}{1-p}\right]_1^{\infty}$. 
			- If $p > 1$ the exponent $1-p$ is negative, so $x^{1-p} \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$: the area is finite and the series converges. 
			- If $p < 1$ the exponent $1-p$ is positive, so $x^{1-p} \to \infty$ as $x \to \infty$: the area is infinite and the series diverges.
		- For $p = 1$: $\int_1^{\infty} \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \left[\ln x\right]_1^{\infty} = \infty$: the area is infinite, and the harmonic series diverges.
- The p-series is the standard yardstick for the (limit) comparison tests, just as the geometric series is the yardstick for the ratio test below.


### Alternating Series

- An **alternating series** is a series whose terms alternate in sign. It can be written as $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n-1} b_n$ (or with $(-1)^n$) where $b_n > 0$ for all $n$.
- **Alternating Series Test** (Leibniz): the alternating series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}b_n$ converges if the following two conditions hold:
	1. $b_{n+1} \leq b_n$ for all $n$ (the magnitudes never increase)
	2. $\lim_{n \to \infty} b_n = 0$.
	- Intuition: the partial sums hop back and forth, each hop shorter than the last, so consecutive partial sums enclose ever-smaller nested intervals that shrink to a single point.
	- Core difference: Regular series with decreasing terms that approach zero are not guaranteed to converge (e.g. harmonic series). An alternating series with decreasing terms that approach zero, will always converge.
- Example: the **alternating harmonic series** $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n} = 1 - \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} - \dots$ converges by the test (its value is $\ln 2$, see the Taylor series section), while the ordinary harmonic series diverges.

### Absolute Convergence, Ratio and Root Tests

- A series $\sum^\infty_{n=1} a_n$ is **absolutely convergent** if the series of absolute values $\sum^\infty_{n=1} |a_n|$ converges. It is **conditionally convergent** if it converges but not absolutely.
	- Example: the alternating harmonic series is conditionally convergent, it converges, but its absolute-value series is the divergent harmonic series.
- **Theorem**: absolute convergence implies convergence (by the comparison test). Consequently, the tests for positive series (integral, comparison, ratio, root) therefore can also be used to decide convergence of sign-mixed series.

### Error Bounds on Partial Sums

When a series converges to $s$ but its value is approximated by a partial sum $s_N$, the **remainder** is the tail $R_N = s - s_N = \sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty} a_n$. The following results bound the approximation error $|R_N|$.

- **Integral test error bound**: under the conditions of the Integral Test (that is, $f$ is continuous, positive, and decreasing, and $a_n = f(n)$), the remainder is trapped between two tail integrals:
	$$
	\int_{N+1}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx \;\leq\; R_N \;\leq\; \int_{N}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx
	$$
	The same rectangles-versus-area picture as in the Integral Test, applied to the tail only.
- **Alternating series error bound**: under the conditions of the Alternating Series Test, the error is at most the first omitted term, and has its sign:
	$$
	|R_N| \leq b_{N+1}
	$$
	Intuition: the next hop overshoots the limit, and all later hops stay within it.
- **Comparison error bound**: if $0 \leq a_n \leq b_n$ for all $n > N$ and $\sum^\infty_{n=1} b_n$ converges, then the remainders compare the same way as the series:
	$$
	R_N = \sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty} a_n \;\leq\; \sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty} b_n
	$$
	This is useful when the comparison series has a tail that can be computed exactly, such as a geometric series.
