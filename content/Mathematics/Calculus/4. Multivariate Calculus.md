### Introduction

Multivariate calculus studies functions of several variables. The core ideas of [[1. Univariate Calculus]] including limits, derivatives, and integrals, still apply, but the extra dimensions change their character. To reduce the amount of duplicate information, we explain how the multivariate case differs from the univariate case, or provide an informal definition.

### From a line to a space

- A **scalar field** (scalar-valued function) is a function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ mapping a point $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ to a single number.
- A **vector field** (vector-valued function) is a function $\mathbf{F}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ with component functions $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = (F_1(\mathbf{x}), \dots, F_m(\mathbf{x}))$. 
- **Distance** in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is measured with the **Euclidean norm** $\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{a}\| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - a_i)^2}$. 
- The neighborhood "$x$ near $a$" that was an **interval** $(a - \delta,\, a + \delta)$ on the line becomes an **open ball** $B_\delta(\mathbf{a}) = \{\mathbf{x} : \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{a}\| < \delta\}$ in higher dimensions.

### Limits and continuity

- Core Difference: On the real number line, you can only approach a point $a$ from two sides, so a limit exists when the left-limit and right-limit agree. In $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $n \ge 2$ there are infinitely many directions, and infinitely many curved paths along which $\mathbf{x}$ can approach $\mathbf{a}$.
- **Definition of the Limit**: $\lim_{\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{a}} f(\mathbf{x}) = L$ if for every $\epsilon > 0$ there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for every $\mathbf{x}$ in the domain of $f$,
	$$
	0 < \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{a}\| < \delta \;\implies\; |f(\mathbf{x}) - L| < \epsilon.
	$$
	- This is exactly the 1D $\epsilon$–$\delta$ definition with the punctured interval $0 < |x - a| < \delta$ replaced by this punctured open ball $0 < ||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}||<\delta$.
	- Similarly, the definition of the limit for a vector field $\mathbf{F}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ with limit $\mathbf{L}$, is formed by replacing $|f(\mathbf{x}) - L|$ by the norm $\|\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbf{L}\|$.
- Intuitively, $f(\mathbf{x})$ can be made arbitrarily close to $L$ for every $\mathbf{x}$ in a small enough ball around $\mathbf{a}$ (excluding $\mathbf{a}$ itself). The limit exists only if $f$ approaches the **same** value $L$ along *every* path. This makes multivariate limits easy to *disprove* (only need to find two paths that yield different limits), but hard to *prove*.
- $f$ is **continuous at** $\mathbf{a}$ if $f(\mathbf{a})$ is defined and $\lim_{\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{a}} f(\mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{a})$ 
	- This is the same definition as in 1D, now with the ball-based limit.

### Partial derivatives

- A **partial derivative** measures the rate of change of $f$ as *one* variable varies while the others are held fixed:
	$$
	\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\mathbf{a}) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(\mathbf{a} + h\,\mathbf{e}_i) - f(\mathbf{a})}{h},
	$$
	where $\mathbf{e}_i$ is the $i$-th standard basis vector. This is an ordinary 1D derivative taken along the $x_i$-axis, so every rule from [[1. Univariate Calculus]] applies to it.
- Partial derivatives only probe the $n$ **axis directions**. On their own they say surprisingly little about how $f$ behaves off the axes (see differentiability below).

### Directional derivatives and the gradient

- The **directional derivative** extends this to *any* direction: for a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$,
	$$
	D_{\mathbf{u}} f(\mathbf{a}) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(\mathbf{a} + h\mathbf{u}) - f(\mathbf{a})}{h}.
	$$
	There are infinitely many of these — one per direction — whereas in 1D there were only two (the right and left derivatives).
- The **gradient** collects all $n$ partial derivatives into a single vector:
	$$
	\nabla f(\mathbf{a}) = \left[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}(\mathbf{a}), \; \dots, \; \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}(\mathbf{a}) \right]^\top.
	$$
- When $f$ is differentiable, every directional derivative is recovered from the gradient by $D_{\mathbf{u}} f(\mathbf{a}) = \nabla f(\mathbf{a})^\top \mathbf{u}$. Consequences:
	- $\nabla f(\mathbf{a})$ points in the direction of **steepest ascent** and $-\nabla f(\mathbf{a})$ in the direction of steepest descent (the basis of gradient descent, see [[Non-Linear Optimization]]).
	- $\|\nabla f(\mathbf{a})\|$ is the maximal rate of increase at $\mathbf{a}$.
	- $\nabla f(\mathbf{a})$ is orthogonal to the level set $\{\mathbf{x} : f(\mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{a})\}$ passing through $\mathbf{a}$.

### Differentiability

- **Another key difference:** in 1D, differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous. In higher dimensions a function can have *all* of its partial derivatives at a point and still be **discontinuous** there — the partials only see the axes. So the mere existence of partial derivatives is *not* the right notion of differentiability.
- $f$ is **differentiable at** $\mathbf{a}$ if near $\mathbf{a}$ it is well-approximated by a linear map (its tangent plane):
	$$
	f(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{h}) = f(\mathbf{a}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{a})^\top \mathbf{h} + o(\|\mathbf{h}\|),
	$$
	meaning the approximation error shrinks faster than $\|\mathbf{h}\|$ as $\mathbf{h} \to \mathbf{0}$.
- Easy sufficient condition: if all partial derivatives exist and are continuous near $\mathbf{a}$, then $f$ is differentiable there. Such an $f$ is called **continuously differentiable** ($C^1$).

### Jacobian and Hessian

- For a vector-valued $\mathbf{F}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, the **Jacobian matrix** gathers the partial derivatives of every component:
	$$
	J_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{a}) = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial F_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial F_1}{\partial x_n} \\[1ex] \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\[1ex] \dfrac{\partial F_m}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial F_m}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}.
	$$
	It is the multivariate analogue of the single derivative $f'$: the best linear approximation of $\mathbf{F}$ at $\mathbf{a}$. Its $i$-th row is $\nabla F_i(\mathbf{a})^\top$, so for a scalar field ($m = 1$) the Jacobian is exactly the gradient written as a row.
	- The **chain rule** becomes matrix multiplication of Jacobians: $J_{\mathbf{F} \circ \mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{a}) = J_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{a}))\, J_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{a})$.
	- When $n = m$, the determinant $\det J_{\mathbf{F}}$ (the **Jacobian determinant**) measures the local volume scaling and appears in the change-of-variables formula for multiple integrals.
- For a scalar field $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, the **Hessian matrix** gathers all second-order partial derivatives:
	$$
	\nabla^2 f(\mathbf{a}) = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1^2} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1 \partial x_n} \\[1ex] \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\[1ex] \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n \partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n^2} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}.
	$$
	It is the Jacobian of the gradient $\nabla f$, and plays the role of the second derivative $f''$.
	- **Clairaut's (Schwarz's) Theorem**: if the second-order partials are continuous, the mixed partials are equal, $\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j} = \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_j \partial x_i}$, so the Hessian is **symmetric**.

### Extreme values

- Just as in 1D, interior extrema of a differentiable $f$ occur only at **critical (stationary) points**, where the gradient vanishes: $\nabla f(\mathbf{a}) = \mathbf{0}$ (the multivariate **Fermat's Theorem**).
- The sign of $f''$ that classified 1D critical points is replaced by the **definiteness of the Hessian** $\nabla^2 f(\mathbf{a})$:
	- positive definite $\Rightarrow$ local minimum,
	- negative definite $\Rightarrow$ local maximum,
	- indefinite $\Rightarrow$ **saddle point**: a shape with no 1D analogue, curving up along some directions and down along others.
- The definitions of maximizer/minimizer, maximum/minimum, and supremum/infimum are exactly those from [[1. Univariate Calculus]], now using balls in place of intervals. Minimizing scalar fields, with or without constraints, is the subject of [[Mathematical Optimization]] and [[Non-Linear Optimization]].
