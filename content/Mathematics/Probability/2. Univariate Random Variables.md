### Core Definitions

- A **random variable** $X$ is a real-valued function on a sample space $X : S \to \mathbb{R}$.
	- $X(S)$ is used to denote the set of all possible values of the random variable $X$, i.e., the range of $X$.
- The **cumulative distribution function** (CDF) of a random variable $X$ is the function $F_X(x) = P(X \leq x)$. The CDF provides the cumulative probability up to a certain value and is defined for all random variables.
- The probability $X$ falls within an interval $(a, b]$ is easily found using the CDF as: $P(a \lt X \leq b) = F(b) - F(a)$.
- Properties of the CDF:
	- $\lim_{x \to \infty} F_X(x) = 1$
	- $\lim_{x \to -\infty} F_X(x) = 0$
	- $x_i \leq x_j \to F_X(x_i) ≤ F_X(x_j)$
- A random variable $X$ may be classified based on its CDF as follows:
	- $X$ is **discrete** if there exists a non-negative function $f_{X}(x)$, called the **probability mass function** (**PMF**), that is non-zero for only a finite or countably infinite set of values, such that its CDF can be expressed as $F_{X}(x)=\sum_{t\leq x}f_{X}(t)$ for all real $x$.
	- $X$ is **continuous** if there exists a non-negative function $f_{X}(x)$, called the **probability density function** (**PDF**), such that its CDF can be expressed as $F_{X}(x)=\int^x_{-\infty}f_{X}(t)dt$ for all real $x$.
	- $X$ is **mixed** if its neither discrete or continuous. In this case, its CDF van be formally expressed as a weighted sum of some discrete CDF $F_{d}(x)$ and continuous CDF $F_{c}(x)$, as follows: $F_{X}(x)=\alpha F_{c}(x)+(1-\alpha)F_{d}(x)$, where $0 < \alpha < 1$.
- A probability mass function (PMF) directly assigns probabilities to values, such that $f_{X}(x)=P(X=x)$, whereas for a probability density function (PDF) the area under the curve corresponds to a probability, and the probability of assuming any specific value is zero $P(X=x)=0$. This difference between the PMF and PDF is reflected in their names.
- The **support** of a PDF/PMF $f_{X}(x)$ is the set of all values $x$ for which $f_{X}(x)>0$.
- A PDF/PMF can depend on some **parameter**. A collection of distributions for different values of a parameter is called a **family**.

### Transformations

- If $X$ is a random variable, and $u(x)$ is a real-valued function whose domain includes the range of $X$, then $u(X)$ is a also a random variable, defined by applying the function $u(x)$ to the outcomes of $X$.
- **CDF method**: determines the distribution of $Y = u(X)$ by first determining its Cumulative Distribution Function. 
	1. Define the CDF for $Y$ in terms of $X$: $F_Y(y) = P(Y \leq y) = P(u(X) \leq y)$.
	2. Solve the inequality $u(X) \leq y$, seperating out $X$ such that you can express the probability using the known CDF of $X$ ($F_X(x)$).
	3. Finally, you can derive the PDF/PMF from the CDF of $Y$.
- **One-to-One Transformations**: Suppose the function $y=u(x)$ represents a one-to-one transformation, then:
	- **Discrete Case**: the PDF of $Y$ is simply $f_Y(y) = f_X(u^{-1}(y))$.
	- **Continuous Case**: suppose also the derivative of the inverse function $\frac{d}{dy}u^{-1}(y)$, is continuous and strictly nonzero over the support of $Y$. The PDF of $Y$ is derived by multiplying the evaluated PDF of $X$ by the absolute value of this derivative (the Jacobian): 
		$$
		f_Y(y) = f_X(u^{-1}(y))\left|\frac{d}{dy}u^{-1}(y)\right|
		$$
	- **Not One-to-One:** If the function $y=u(x)$ is not one-to-one, you can partition the domain into disjoint subsets where it is one-to-one, and sum the results over those partitions.

### Expectation

- If $X$ is a random variable with PDF $f(x)$, then the **expected value** of $X$ is given by: 
	- Discrete $X$: $E(X) = \sum_i x_i \cdot f(x_i)$ 
	- Continuous $X$: $E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \cdot f(x)\,  dx$
- **Law of the Unconscious Statistician (LOTUS)**: A theorem used to calculate the expected value of a function $g(X)$ of a random variable $X$, without needing to know the distribution of $g(X)$ itself.
	- Discrete $X$: $E[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)f_X(x)$
	- Continuous $X$: $E[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x)f_X(x) \, dx$
- The expected value or mean is frequently denoted as $\mu$.
- Properties:
    - $E(c) = c$
    - $E(aX + b) = aE(X) + b$
    - $E(a \cdot X + b \cdot Y) = a \cdot E(X) + b \cdot E(Y)$

### Variance

- The **variance** (denoted as $\text{Var}(X)$ or $\sigma^2$) of a random variable $X$ is given by $\text{Var}(X) = E((X - E(X))^2)= E(X^2) - (E(X))^2$.
- The **standard deviation** (denoted as $\sigma$) of a random variable $X$ is defined as the square root of its variance $\sqrt{\text{Var(X)}}$.
- Properties:
    - $Var(X) \geq 0$
    - $Var(aX + b) = a^2 \cdot Var(X)$

### Moments

- The $k^\text{th}$ **moment** $\mu'_k$ of a random variable $X$ is defined as $E(X^k)$.
    - Also called **moment about the origin**.
    - $\mu'_{1}=E(X)$
- The $k^\text{th}$ **central moment** $\mu_k$ of a random variable $X$ is defined as $E( (X - \mu)^k )$
    - Also called **moment about the mean**.
    - $\mu_{1}=0$
    - $\mu_{2}=Var(X)$

- **Skewness** is the third standardized moment, measuring the asymmetry of the probability distribution about its mean.
	$$
	\text{Skew}[X] = E\left[\left(\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^3\right] = \frac{\mu_3}{\sigma^3}
	$$
- **Kurtosis** is the fourth standardized moment, measuring the "tailedness" (or outlier propensity) of the probability distribution.
    $$
    \text{Kurt}[X] = E\left[\left(\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^4\right] = \frac{\mu_4}{\sigma^4}
    $$


### Moment-Generating Function (MGF)

- If $X$ is a random variable then the expected value $M_X(t) = E(e^{tX})$ is called the **moment generating function** (**MGF**) of $X$. But only if the expectation exists for all values of $t$ within some interval $|t| < h$ for some $h > 0$.
- The $r$-th derivative of the moment generating function $M_X(t)$ at $t=0$ equals the $r$-th moment $u'_r$.
- Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables such that $Y = aX + b$ for some constants $a$ and $b$, then $M_Y(t) = e^{bt} \cdot M_X(at)$.
