### Markov's Inequality

Theorem: For a random variable $X$ that only takes non-negative values, it holds that for every $c > 0$:
$$
P(X \geq c) \leq \frac{E(X)}{c}
$$

Proof:
1. Fix $c > 0$ and consider the indicator $\mathbf{1}_{\{X \geq c\}}$, which is $1$ when $X \geq c$ and $0$ otherwise. 
2. Since $X \geq 0$, we know $X \geq X \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\{ X\geq c\}}$.
3. In the event $\{X \geq c\}$ the factor $X$ is at least $c$, therefore $X \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\{X \geq c\}} \geq c \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\{X \geq c\}}$.
4. Taking expectations and using monotonicity and linearity yields:
	$$
	E(X) \;\geq\; c \cdot E\big(\mathbf{1}_{\{X \geq c\}}\big) \;=\; c \cdot P(X \geq c).
	$$
5. Dividing by $c > 0$ gives $P(X \geq c) \leq E(X)/c$. $\quad\blacksquare$

### Chebyshev's Inequality

Theorem: For every random variable $X$ with expected value $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 > 0$ it holds that for every $k > 0$:
	$$
	P(|X - \mu| \geq k\sigma) \leq \frac{1}{k^2}
	$$
- Consequently, $P(|X - \mu| \lt k\sigma) \geq 1 - 1 / k^2$.
- Equivalently, writing $\epsilon = k\sigma$, for every $\epsilon > 0$: $P(|X - \mu| \geq \epsilon) \leq \sigma^2 / \epsilon^2$.

Proof: 
1. Take the non-negative random variable $Y = (X - \mu)^2$.
2. Apply Markov's inequality for any $a > 0$:
	$$
	P(Y \geq a) \leq \frac{E(Y)}{a} \iff P((X-\mu)^2 \geq a)\leq\frac{E\big((X-\mu)^2\big)}{a} = \frac{\sigma^2}{a}.
	$$
3. Choose $a = (k\sigma)^2 = k^2 \sigma^2$, since squaring is monotone on non-negative numbers:
	$$
	(X - \mu)^2 \geq k^2\sigma^2 \iff |X - \mu| \geq k\sigma.
	$$
4. Substituting 3. into the formula from 2., we get:
	$$
	P(|X - \mu| \geq k\sigma) = P\big((X-\mu)^2 \geq k^2\sigma^2\big) \leq \frac{\sigma^2}{k^2\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{k^2}. \qquad \blacksquare
	$$

### Modes of Convergence

- **Convergence in Probability** (**plim**): A sequence of random variables $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ converges in probability to a random variable $X$ if, for every margin $\epsilon > 0$, the probability that the distance between $X_n$ and $X$ is greater than $\epsilon$ approaches zero as $n \to \infty$:
	$$
	\lim_{n \to \infty} P(|X_n - X| \geq \epsilon) = 0
	$$
	Notation: $X_n \xrightarrow{p} X$ or $\text{plim}_{n \to \infty} X_n = X$.
- **Convergence Almost Surely** (**a.s.**): A sequence of random variables $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ converges almost surely to a random variable $X$ if the probability that the actual sequence of outcomes approaches $X$ is exactly 1:
	$$
	P\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} X_n = X\right) = 1
	$$
	Notation: $X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} X$.
- **Convergence in Distribution**: A sequence of random variables $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ with cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) $F_n(x)$ converges in distribution to a random variable $X$ with CDF $F(x)$ if the CDFs converge at every point $x$ where $F(x)$ is continuous:
	$$
	\lim_{n \to \infty} F_n(x) = F(x)
	$$
	Notation: $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$.
- **Hierarchy of Convergence:** Convergence almost surely is the strongest condition, implying convergence in probability, which in turn implies convergence in distribution:
    $$ 
    X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} X \implies X_n \xrightarrow{p} X \implies X_n \xrightarrow{d} X
    $$
    Note: The reverse is generally false, with one notable exception: if $X_n \xrightarrow{d} c$, where $c$ is a constant, then $X_n \xrightarrow{p} c$.
- **Continuous Mapping Theorem (CMT):** If $X_n$ converges to $X$ (in probability, almost surely, or in distribution) and $g(\cdot)$ is a continuous function, then $g(X_n)$ converges to $g(X)$ in that exact same mode.
- **Slutsky's Theorem:** If $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ and $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} c$ (where $c$ is a constant), you can algebraically combine them:
    - Addition: $X_n + Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X + c$
    - Multiplication: $X_n Y_n \xrightarrow{d} cX$
    - Division: $X_n / Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X / c$ (provided $c \neq 0$)

### Limit Theorems

- Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with finite expected value $E(X_i) = \mu$, and finite variance $Var(X_{i})=\sigma^2 > 0$. 
	- Note: finite variance is only a strict requirement for the CLT, but not necessary for WLLN, and SLLN. For the proofs of WLLN and SLLN we will however rely on this assumption.
- The **sample mean** for a sample of size $n$ is defined as:
	$$
	\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i
	$$
	- **Mean:** $E(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n E(X_i) = \frac{n\mu}{n} = \mu$
	- **Variance:** $\text{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \text{Var}(X_i) = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$
- The **Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN)** states that the sample mean converges _in probability_ to the expected value $\mu$ as the sample size grows. For any margin $\epsilon > 0$:
	$$
	\lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \geq \epsilon) = 0
	$$
- The **Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN)** states that the sample mean converges _almost surely_ to the expected value $\mu$ as the sample size grows. This is a stricter mathematical condition than the weak law, meaning the probability that the limit of the sample mean exactly equals $\mu$ is 1:
	$$
	P\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} \bar{X}_n = \mu\right) = 1
	$$
- The **Central Limit Theorem (CLT)** states that the sample mean converges _in distribution_ to a normal distribution as the sample size grows. When standardized, the sample mean approaches a standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$:
	$$
	\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
	$$

### Proof of the Weak Law of Large Numbers

Outline:
- Assumptions: $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ are i.i.d. with finite mean $E(X_i) = \mu$ and finite variance $\text{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2 < \infty$.
	- The finite-variance assumption is what makes this short proof work. The WLLN in fact holds under only a finite mean (Khinchin's theorem), but that stronger result needs a characteristic-function argument.
- To show: $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$, that is, for every $\epsilon > 0$, $\lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \geq \epsilon) = 0$
- Approach: Apply Chebyshev's inequality directly to the sample mean, using $E(\bar{X}_n) = \mu$ and $\text{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \sigma^2 / n$.
	
Proof:
1. The sample mean has mean $E(\bar{X}_n) = \mu$ and variance $\text{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \sigma^2/n$.
2. Applying Chebyshev's inequality to $\bar{X}_n$ in the form $P(|Y - E(Y)| \geq \epsilon) \leq \text{Var}(Y)/\epsilon^2$, we get for every $\epsilon > 0$:
	$$
	P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \geq \epsilon) \leq \frac{\text{Var}(\bar{X}_n)}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}.
	$$
3. With $\epsilon$ and $\sigma^2$ fixed, the right-hand side tends to $0$ as $n \to \infty$. Since a probability is bounded below by $0$, the squeeze theorem gives
	$$
	0 \leq \lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \geq \epsilon) \leq \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2} = 0.
	$$
4. Hence the limit is $0$ for every $\epsilon > 0$, which is exactly $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$. $\quad \blacksquare$
