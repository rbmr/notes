Usually, many of these terms are introduced for the case $n=2$ first, and then generalized to $n>2$ later, but since I dont like redundancy, I am tackling $n\geq 2$ all at once.

### Random Vectors

- A **random vector** $\mathbf{X} = [X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n]^T$ is a column vector where each element $X_i$ is a scalar random variable. A specific realized value of this vector is denoted by the lowercase $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)^T$.
- The **expected value** (or mean vector) of a random vector is the vector of the expected values of its components: $\boldsymbol{\mu} = E[\mathbf{X}] = [E[X_1], E[X_2], \ldots, E[X_n]]^T$.
- For a random vector $\mathbf{X}$, a constant matrix $A$, and a constant vector $\mathbf{b}$, the expectation operator is linear: $E[A\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}] = A E[\mathbf{X}] + \mathbf{b}$.

### Joint Distributions

- The **joint cumulative distribution function** (**joint CDF**) of a random vector $\mathbf{X}$ is a function $F_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})$ that accepts a vector $\mathbf{x} = [x_1, \ldots, x_n]^T$ and gives the probability that all random components are simultaneously less than or equal to their respective vector elements: $F_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}) = P(X_1 \leq x_1, X_2 \leq x_2, \ldots, X_n \leq x_n)$.
- The **joint probability density/mass function** (**joint PDF/PMF**) $f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})$ evaluates probabilities (or density) for a given vector $\mathbf{x}$.
	- Discrete: The joint PMF yields the exact probability of the vector occurring: $f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}) = P(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{x}) = P(X_1=x_1, \ldots, X_n=x_n)$.
	- Continuous: The joint PDF is a function whose multiple integral over a specific multi-dimensional region yields the probability of the random vector falling into that region. It relates to the joint CDF by $f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\partial^n}{\partial x_1 \ldots \partial x_n} F_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})$.
- The **support** of a joint distribution is the set of all vectors $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ for which the joint probability density or mass function is strictly positive, $f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}) \gt 0$.
- The **joint moment generating function** (**joint MGF**) for a random vector $\mathbf{X}$ evaluated at a constant vector $\mathbf{t} = [t_1, \ldots, t_n]^T$ is $M_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{t}) = E[e^{\mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{X}}] = E[\exp(t_1X_1 + \ldots + t_nX_n)]$.

### Independence

- A random vector $\mathbf{X} = [X_1, \ldots, X_n]^T$ can be partitioned into two sub-vectors, $\mathbf{X}_1$ and $\mathbf{X}_2$, such that $\mathbf{X} = [\mathbf{X}_1^T, \mathbf{X}_2^T]^T$. For this section, the ordering of random variables is irrelevant, $\mathbf{X}_1$ can be any subset of random variables from $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{X}_2$ is simply the remaining random variables.
- The **marginal distribution** of $\mathbf{X}_1$ is obtained by "integrating/summing out" the variables in $\mathbf{X}_2$ from the joint distribution of $\mathbf{X}$:
	- Discrete: The marginal PMF is $f_{\mathbf{X}_1}(\mathbf{x}_1) = \sum_{\mathbf{x}_2} f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2)$
	- Continuous: The marginal PDF is $f_{\mathbf{X}_1}(\mathbf{x}_1) = \int f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2) \, d\mathbf{x}_2$ (where the integral is taken over the entire multi-dimensional space of $\mathbf{x}_2$).
	- Note that a single random vector may contain a combination of discrete and continuous random variables, which requires a combination of the above.
- The **conditional distribution** of $\mathbf{X}_1$ given $\mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2$ is defined by dividing the joint distribution by the marginal distribution of $\mathbf{X}_2$ (provided $f_{\mathbf{X}_2}(\mathbf{x}_2) \gt 0$):
	$$
	f_{\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2}(\mathbf{x}_1 | \mathbf{x}_2) = \frac{f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2)}{f_{\mathbf{X}_2}(\mathbf{x}_2)}
	$$
- Two random vectors $\mathbf{X}_1$ and $\mathbf{X}_2$ are **independent** if their joint distribution is exactly equal to the product of their marginal distributions for all possible values of $\mathbf{x} = [\mathbf{x}_1^T, \mathbf{x}_2^T]^T$:
	$$
	f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}) = f_{\mathbf{X}_1}(\mathbf{x}_1) f_{\mathbf{X}_2}(\mathbf{x}_2)
	$$
- All the scalar random variables $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ within a random vector $\mathbf{X}$ are **mutually independent** if their joint distribution is equal to the product of their individual marginal distributions for all possible values of the vector $\mathbf{x} = [x_1, \ldots, x_n]^T$:
	$$
	f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^n f_{X_i}(x_i)
	$$
- The random variables in a random vector $\mathbf{X}$ are **independent and identically distributed** (i.i.d.) if they are mutually independent and every component $X_i$ follows the exact same marginal probability distribution.

### Covariance

- The **covariance** $\text{Cov}(X, Y)$ between two random variables $X$ and $Y$, is given by $\text{Cov}(X,Y)=E[(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])]=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]$.
- Properties:
	- $\text{Cov}(X, X) = \text{Var}(X)$
	- $\text{Cov}(X, Y) = \text{Cov}(Y, X)$
	- $\text{Cov}(aX + b, cY + d) = ac \cdot \text{Cov}(X, Y)$  (provided $a, c > 0$)
	- If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then $\text{Cov}(X,Y)=0$
		- note the reverse is not always true.
	- $\text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y) + 2\text{Cov}(X, Y)$
- The **covariance matrix** (or variance-covariance matrix), denoted $\Sigma$, for a random vector $\mathbf{X} = [X_1, \ldots, X_n]^T$ is an $n \times n$ symmetric matrix where the $(i, j)$-th entry is $\text{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$. The diagonal entries are the variances $\text{Var}(X_i)$. It is calculated as $\Sigma = E[(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^T]$.
-  For a random vector $\mathbf{X}$, a constant matrix $A$, and a constant vector $\mathbf{b}$, the variance of a linear transformation is: $\text{Var}(A\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}) = A \text{Var}(\mathbf{X}) A^T$.

### Correlation

- The **correlation** $\rho_{X,Y}$ between two random variables $X$ and $Y$, is given by $\rho_{X,Y} = \text{Corr}(X, Y) = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}$.
- Properties:
	- $-1 \leq \rho_{X,Y} \leq 1$ where:
		- $\rho = 1$: Perfect positive linear relationship.
		- $\rho = -1$: Perfect negative linear relationship.
		- $\rho = 0$: No linear relationship (variables are uncorrelated).
	- $\text{Corr}(X, X) = 1$
	- $\text{Corr}(X, Y) = \text{Corr}(Y, X)$
	- $\text{Corr}(aX + b, cY + d) = \text{Corr}(X, Y)$ (provided $a, c > 0$)
	- If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then $\text{Corr}(X, Y) = 0$.
		- note the reverse is not always true.
- The **correlation matrix**, denoted $P$ or $R$, for a random vector $\mathbf{X} = [X_1, \ldots, X_n]^T$ is an $n \times n$ symmetric matrix where the $(i, j)$-th entry is the correlation $\rho_{X_i, X_j} = \text{Corr}(X_i, X_j)$. The diagonal entries are always 1. 
	- It can be calculated from the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ using the diagonal matrix of standard deviations $D = \text{diag}(\sigma_{X_1}, \ldots, \sigma_{X_n})$. The correlation matrix is given by $P = D^{-1} \Sigma D^{-1}$.

### Conditional Expectation

- The **conditional expectation** (or conditional mean) of a random vector $\mathbf{X}_1$ given that $\mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2$ is the expected value of $\mathbf{X}_1$ evaluated using the conditional distribution $f_{\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2}(\mathbf{x}_1 | \mathbf{x}_2)$.
    - Discrete: $E[\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2] = \sum_{\mathbf{x}_1} \mathbf{x}_1 f_{\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2}(\mathbf{x}_1 | \mathbf{x}_2)$
    - Continuous: $E[\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2] = \int \mathbf{x}_1 f_{\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2}(\mathbf{x}_1 | \mathbf{x}_2) \, d\mathbf{x}_1$
- The difference between $E[\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2]$ and $E[\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2]$ is as follows:
    - $E[\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2]$ is a **deterministic vector**; it is a fixed value computed for a specific, known realization $\mathbf{x}_2$.
    - $E[\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2]$ is a **random vector**; it is a function of the random vector $\mathbf{X}_2$ and its value remains unknown until $\mathbf{X}_2$ is observed.
- **Law of Total Expectation** (Tower Property): The unconditional expected value of a random vector $\mathbf{X}_1$ is equal to the expected value of its conditional expectation given $\mathbf{X}_2$:
	$$
	E[\mathbf{X}_1] = E[E[\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2]]
	$$

### Conditional Variance

- The **conditional variance** (specifically, the conditional covariance matrix) of a random vector $\mathbf{X}_1$ given that $\mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2$ is defined as the expected value of the outer product of the deviation of $\mathbf{X}_1$ from its conditional mean, evaluated using the conditional distribution $f_{\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2}(\mathbf{x}_1 | \mathbf{x}_2)$:
	$$
	\text{Var}(\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2) = E[(\mathbf{X}_1 - E[\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2])(\mathbf{X}_1 - E[\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2])^T | \mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2]
	$$
- The difference between $\text{Var}(\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2)$ and $\text{Var}(\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2)$ is as follows:
    - $\text{Var}(\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{x}_2)$ is a **deterministic matrix**; it is a fixed covariance matrix calculated for a specific, known realization vector $\mathbf{x}_2$.
    - $\text{Var}(\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2)$ is a **random matrix**; it is a function of the random vector $\mathbf{X}_2$, meaning its realized values remain unknown until $\mathbf{X}_2$ is observed.
- **Law of Total Variance**: The unconditional variance (covariance matrix) of a random vector $\mathbf{X}_1$ can be decomposed into the expected value of the conditional variance plus the variance of the conditional expectation:
	$$
	\text{Var}(\mathbf{X}_1) = E[\text{Var}(\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2)] + \text{Var}(E[\mathbf{X}_1 | \mathbf{X}_2])
	$$
