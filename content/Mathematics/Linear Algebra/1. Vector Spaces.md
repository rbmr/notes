### Fields

- A **field** $\mathbf{F}$ is a set provided with two binary operations, called **addition** ($+: \mathbf{F} \times \mathbf{F} \to \mathbf{F}$) and **multiplication** ($*: \mathbf{F} \times \mathbf{F} \to \mathbf{F}$), such that the following axioms hold:

|**Property**|**Description**|
|---|---|
|Commutativity|$\alpha + \beta = \beta + \alpha$ and $\alpha\beta = \beta\alpha$ for all $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbf{F}$|
|Associativity|$(\alpha + \beta) + \lambda = \alpha + (\beta + \lambda)$ and $(\alpha\beta)\lambda = \alpha(\beta\lambda)$ for all $\alpha, \beta, \lambda \in \mathbf{F}$|
|Identities|There exist distinct elements $0, 1 \in \mathbf{F}$ such that $\lambda + 0 = \lambda$ and $\lambda \cdot 1 = \lambda$ for all $\lambda \in \mathbf{F}$|
|Additive Inverse|For every $\alpha \in \mathbf{F}$, there exists a unique additive inverse $(-\alpha) \in \mathbf{F}$ such that $\alpha + (-\alpha) = 0$|
|Multiplicative Inverse|For every $\alpha \in \mathbf{F}$ with $\alpha \neq 0$, there exists a unique multiplicative inverse $(1/\alpha) \in \mathbf{F}$ such that $\alpha(1/\alpha) = 1$|
|Distributive Property|$\lambda(\alpha + \beta) = \lambda\alpha + \lambda\beta$ for all $\lambda, \alpha, \beta \in \mathbf{F}$|

- Elements of a field are called **scalars**. 
- Common examples of fields include the sets of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$, real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ and complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$.
- Suppose we have some field $\mathbf{F}$, then:
	- For any $\beta, \alpha \in \mathbb{F}$, **subtraction** is defined as addition with the additive inverse: $\beta - \alpha = \beta + (-\alpha)$.
	- For any $\beta \in \mathbb{F}$ and any non-zero $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}$, **division** is defined as multiplication with the multiplicative inverse: $\beta / \alpha = \beta(1/\alpha)$.

### Vector Spaces

- A **vector space** $V$ over a field $\mathbf{F}$ is a set with two binary operations, called **vector addition** ($+: \mathbf{V} \times \mathbf{V} \to \mathbf{V}$) and **scalar multiplication** ($\cdot: \mathbf{F} \times \mathbf{V} \to \mathbf{V}$), such that the following axioms hold:

|**Property**|**Description**|
|---|---|
|Commutativity|$u + v = v + u$ for all $u, v \in V$|
|Associativity|$(u + v) + w = u + (v + w)$ and $(ab)v = a(bv)$ for all $u, v, w \in V$ and $a, b \in \mathbf{F}$|
|Additive Identity|There exists an element $0 \in V$ such that $v + 0 = v$ for all $v \in V$|
|Additive Inverse|For every $v \in V$, there exists a $w \in V$ such that $v + w = 0$|
|Multiplicative Identity|There exists an element $1 \in \mathbf{F}$ such that $1v = v$ for all $v \in V$|
|Distributive Properties|$a(u + v) = au + av$ and $(a + b)v = av + bv$ for all $a, b \in \mathbf{F}$ and $u, v \in V$|

- Elements of a vector space are called **vectors** or **points**. The additive identity of a vector space is also called its **zero vector**.
- A vector space over $\mathbb{R}$ is called a **real vector space**. A vector space over $\mathbb{C}$ is called a **complex vector space**.
- Using the axioms for vector spaces we may derive the following properties:
	- Each vector space has exactly one additive identity $0$.
	- Every vector $v \in V$ has exactly one additive inverse $-v$.
	- For $0 \in \mathbf{F}$ and any vector $v \in V$: $0v = 0$.
	- For $0 \in V$ and any scalar $a \in \mathbf{F}$: $a0 = 0$.
	- For any vector $v \in V$: $(-1)v = -v$.

### Lists as Vectors

- $\mathbf{F}^n$ is a vector space over $\mathbf{F}$, under the following definitions of vector addition and scalar multiplication.
	- For any two vectors $u = (u_1, \dots, u_n)$ and $v = (v_1, \dots, v_n)$ in $\mathbf{F}^n$, their sum is: $u + v = (u_1 + v_1, u_2 + v_2, \dots, u_n + v_n)$.
	- The additive identity becomes the zero vector $0 = (0, 0, \dots, 0)$, and the additive inverse becomes $-v = (-x_1, \dots, -x_n)$.
	- For any scalar $a \in \mathbf{F}$ and any vector $v = (v_1, \dots, v_n) \in \mathbf{F}^n$, the product is: $av = (av_1, av_2, \dots, av_n)$.

### Polynomials as Vectors

- A **polynomial** with coefficients in $\mathbf{F}$ is a function $p: \mathbf{F} \to \mathbf{F}$ of the form $p(z) = a_0 + a_1z + a_2z^2 + \dots + a_mz^m$, where $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_m \in \mathbf{F}$.
- The **degree** of a polynomial $p$, denoted $\deg p$, is the highest power of $z$ that has a non-zero coefficient. For example, if $a_m \neq 0$ and $a_k = 0$ for all $k > m$, then $\deg p = m$. By convention, the identically zero polynomial $p(z) = 0$ is said to have a degree of $-\infty$.
- The set of all polynomials with coefficients in $\mathbf{F}$ is denoted by $\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{F})$. Under the standard operations of function addition and scalar multiplication, $\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{F})$ forms a vector space over $\mathbf{F}$.
- The set of all polynomials with coefficients in $\mathbf{F}$ and a degree of at most $m$ is denoted by $\mathcal{P}_m(\mathbf{F})$. Under the standard operations of function addition and scalar multiplication, $\mathcal{P}_m(\mathbf{F})$ is a valid subspace of $\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{F})$, making it a vector space itself.

### Subspaces

- $U$ is a **subspace** of $V$ if and only if
	1. $U$ is a subset of $V$, 
	2. $U$ is a vector space using the same vector addition, scalar multiplication and field as $V$,
	3. the zero vector of $V$ is also in $U$.
- The smallest possible subspace of $V$ is the set $\{0\}$, the largest subspace is $V$ itself.
- The **sum** of subspaces $V_1, \dots, V_m$, denoted by $V_1 + \dots + V_m$, is the set of all possible sums of their elements: 
	$$
	V_1 + \dots + V_m = \{v_1 + \dots + v_m : v_1 \in V_1, \dots, v_m \in V_m\}
	$$
- A subspace sum $V_1 + \dots + V_m$ is a **direct sum** (denoted $V_1 \oplus \dots \oplus V_m$) if every element of the sum can be written in only one way as a sum $v_1 + \dots + v_m$, where each $v_k \in V_k$.
- Equivalent conditions for a direct sum:
	- A sum $V_1 + \dots + V_m$ is a direct sum if and only if the only way to write the zero vector as a sum $v_1 + \dots + v_m$ is if $v_k = 0$ for all $k$.
	- For two subspaces $U$ and $W$ of $V$, their sum $U + W$ is a **direct sum** if and only if their intersection contains only the zero vector.

### Span and Linear Dependence

- A **linear combination** of a list of vectors $v_{1}, \dots, v_{m}$ in $V$ is a vector  $u \in V$ of the form $u=a_{1}v_{1} + \dots + a_{m}v_{m}$, where $a_{1}, \dots, a_{m} \in F$.
- The **span** of a list of vectors $v_{1}, \dots, v_{m}$, denoted $\text{span}(v_{1}, \dots, v_{m})$, is the set of all possible linear combinations of that list.
	- The span of an empty list $()$ is defined as $\{0\}$.
	- If $\text{span}(v_{1}, \dots, v_{m})$ equals $V$, the list is said to **span** $V$.
- The span of a list of vectors in $V$ is the smallest subspace of $V$ that contains all the vectors in the list.
- A list of vectors $v_{1}, \dots, v_{m}$ is **linearly independent** if the only choice of scalars $a_{1}, \dots, a_{m} \in F$ that makes $a_{1}v_{1} + \dots + a_{m}v_{m} = 0$ is $a_{1} = \dots = a_{m} = 0$.
	- The empty list $()$ is defined to be linearly independent.
	- A list of vectors is **linearly dependent** if it is not linearly independent.
- *Linear Dependence Lemma*: If a list $v_{1}, v_{2}, \dots, v_{m}$ is linearly dependent, at least one vector $v_{k}$ is in the span of the previous vectors ($v_{1}, \dots, v_{k-1}$). Removing this vector does not change the span of the original list.

### Bases

- A **basis** of $V$ is a list of vectors in $V$ that is both **linearly independent** and **spans** $V$.
- Equivalent condition for a basis: A list $v_{1}, \dots, v_{n}$ is a basis of $V$ if and only if every $u \in V$ can be written uniquely as a linear combination $u = a_{1}v_{1} + \dots + a_{n}v_{n}$.
- Key properties of bases:
	- *Reduction*: Every list spanning a vector space $V$ can be reduced to a basis of $V$ by removing redundant vectors.
	- *Extension*: Every linearly independent list of vectors from a finite-dimensional vector space $V$ can be extended to a basis of $V$ by adjoining necessary vectors.
	- *Existence*: Every finite-dimensional vector space has a basis.
- If $V$ is finite-dimensional and $U$ is a subspace of $V$, there exists a subspace $W$ of $V$ such that $V = U \oplus W$. This is proven by extending a basis of $U$ to a basis of $V$, the span of the added vectors forms $W$.

### Dimensions

- A vector space is **finite-dimensional** if there exists some finite list of vectors that spans the space. A vector space is **infinite-dimensional** if it is not finite-dimensional.
- For any finite-dimensional vector space $V$, the length of a linearly independent list of vectors from $V$ is always less than or equal to the length of any list of vectors spanning $V$.
- All bases of a finite-dimensional vector space have the same length.
- The **dimension** of a finite-dimensional vector space $V$, denoted $\dim V$, is the length of any basis of $V$.
- If $U$ is a subspace of a finite-dimensional vector space $V$, then $\dim U \le \dim V$. If $\dim U = \dim V$, then $U = V$.
- *Right length theorems*: If you know the dimension of a vector space $V$ is $n$, then:
	- Any linearly independent list of vectors from $V$ of length $n$ is a basis of $V$.
	- Any list of vectors of length $n$ spanning $V$ is a basis of $V$.
- For any two subspaces $V_1$ and $V_2$ of a finite-dimensional vector space: $\dim(V_1 + V_2) = \dim V_1 + \dim V_2 - \dim(V_1 \cap V_2)$
