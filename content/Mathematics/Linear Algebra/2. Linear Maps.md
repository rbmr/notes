### Linear Maps

- A **linear map** (also called a **linear transformation**) from a vector space $V$ to a vector space $W$ is a function $T: V \rightarrow W$ that satisfies two key properties: 
	1. additivity: $T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v)$ for all $u, v \in V$
	2. homogeneity: $T(\lambda v) = \lambda(T(v))$ for all $\lambda \in F$ and $v \in V$
- The set of all linear maps from $V$ to $W$ is denoted by $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$.
- An **operator** is a linear map $T: V \to V$ from a vector space $V$ to itself.  
- The set of all linear operators on $V$ is denoted by $\mathcal{L}(V)$ which is shorthand for $\mathcal{L}(V, V)$.
- The **identity operator**, denoted by $I$, is the unique operator that takes each element to itself. Mathematically: $I \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is defined by $Iv = v$ for all $v \in V$.
- The **zero map** $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ is the unique operator that takes each vector $v\in V$ to the zero vector $0 \in W$. Mathematically: $T(v)=0$ for all $v\in V$.
- The **zero operator** is a linear map that is both a zero map, and an operator.

Some properties of linear maps:

- Every linear map $T \in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$ takes the zero vector of $V$ to the zero vector of $W$, meaning $T(0) = 0$.
- Given a basis $v_1, \dots, v_n$ of $V$ and arbitrary vectors $w_1, \dots, w_n \in W$, there exists a unique linear map $T: V \rightarrow W$ such that $T(v_k) = w_k$ for each $k = 1, \dots, n$.

### Linear Maps as Vectors

- If $V$ and $W$ are vector spaces over $\mathbf{F}$, then $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ is also a vector space with the following operations:
	- Addition: For $S, T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$, the sum $S + T$ is the linear map defined by $(S + T)(v) = S(v) + T(v)$ for all $v \in V$.
	- Scalar Multiplication: For $\lambda \in \mathbf{F}$ and $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$, the product $\lambda T$ is the linear map defined by $(\lambda T)(v) = \lambda(T(v))$ for all $v \in V$.
- The additive identity of the vector space $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ is the zero map.

### Products of Linear Maps

- If $T \in \mathcal{L}(U, V)$ and $S \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$, their **product** $ST \in \mathcal{L}(U, W)$ is defined by the composition $(ST)(u) = S(T(u))$ for all $u \in U$.
- Suppose $T_1, T_2 \in \mathcal{L}(U, V)$, $S_1, S_2 \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$, and $R \in \mathcal{L}(W, X)$, then the following properties hold.
	- Associativity: $(RS)T = R(ST)$.
	- Identity: Every vector space $V$ has an identity map $I_V \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ where $I_V(v) = v$ for all $v \in V$. Multiplying by the identity map leaves a linear map unchanged: $TI_U = T$ and $I_WT = T$.
	- Distributivity: The product of linear maps distributes over addition. 
		- Right distributivity: $(S_1 + S_2)T_1 = S_1T_1 + S_2T_1$. 
		- Left distributivity: $S_1(T_1 + T_2) = S_1T_1 + S_1T_2$.
	- Non-commutativity: The product of linear maps is not commutative in general. It is frequently true that $ST \neq TS$.
- The **power of a linear map**: For an operator $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and a positive integer $m$, the power $T^m$ is defined as the product of $T$ with itself $m$ times ($T^m = T T\dots T$). By convention, $T^0$ is defined as the identity operator $I$ on $V$.
- A nilpotent operator is an operator such that some power of it equals the zero map. Mathematically: $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is nilpotent if there exists a positive integer $m$ such that $T^m = 0$

### Null spaces and Ranges

Recap on the definitions of injective, range, surjective, etc: [[Set Theory#Functions]].

- The **null space** of a linear map  $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ (denoted $\text{null } T$) is the subset of $V$ containing all vectors $v \in V$ that $T$ maps to $0 \in W$. Mathematically: $\text{null } T = \{v \in V : T(v) = 0\}$.
	- $\text{null }T$ is always a subspace of the domain $V$.
- A linear map $T$ is injective if and only if its null space contains only the zero vector.
- The range of $T$ is a subspace of the target space $W$.
- If $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space and $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$, then the range of $T$ is finite-dimensional, and the dimension of the domain equals the sum of the dimensions of the null space and the range: $\dim V = \dim \text{null } T + \dim \text{range } T$.
- If $V$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional vector spaces, 
	- and $\dim V > \dim W$, then no linear map from $V$ to $W$ can be injective.
	- and $\dim V < \dim W$, then no linear map from $V$ to $W$ can be surjective.
	- and $\dim V = \dim W$, then for any linear map $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$, the following statements are equivalent:
		1. $T$ is injective.
		2. $T$ is surjective.
		3. $T$ is invertible (bijective).

### Invertibility

- A linear map $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ is **invertible** if there exists a linear map $T^{-1} \in \mathcal{L}(W, V)$, called the **inverse** of $T$, such that $T^{-1}T = I_V$ and $TT^{-1} = I_W$.
- If a linear map is invertible, its inverse is unique.
- A linear map is invertible if and only if it is **bijective** (both injective and surjective).
- An **isomorphism** is an invertible linear map.
- Two vector spaces $V$ and $W$ are **isomorphic** if there exists an isomorphism between them.
- Two finite-dimensional vector spaces over the same field are isomorphic if and only if $\dim V = \dim W$.
- If $T$ is invertible with inverse $T^{-1}$, then $T^{-m} \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is defined by $T^{-m} = (T^{-1})^m$. 

### Eigenvalues

- Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$.  A subspace $U$ of $V$ is called **invariant** under $T$ if $Tu \in U$ for all $u \in U$.
- Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. A number $\lambda \in \mathbf{F}$ is called an **eigenvalue** if there exists a $v \in V$ such that $v \neq 0$ and $Tv=\lambda v$.
- Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\lambda \in \mathbf{F}$ is an eigenvalue of $T$. A vector $v \in V$ is called an **eigenvector** of $T$ corresponding to $\lambda$ if $v \neq 0$ and $Tv = \lambda v$.
- Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. Then every list of every list of eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigen values of $T$ is linearly independent.
- Suppose $V$ is finite-dimensional. Then each operator on $V$ has at most $\text{dim }V$ distinct eigenvalues.
- Every operator on a finite-dimensional nonzero complex vector space has an eigenvalue.
- Every operator on an odd-dimensional vector space has an eigenvalue.
- Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\lambda \in \mathbf{F}$. The **eigenspace** of $T$ corresponding to $\lambda$ is the subspace $E(\lambda,T)$ of $V$ defined by:
	$$
	E(\lambda, T)=\text{null}(T-\lambda I) = \{v \in V \mid Tv=\lambda v\}
	$$
- Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$ are distinct eigenvalues of $T$. Then the sum of the corresponding eigenspaces is a direct sum: $E(\lambda_1, T) \oplus \dots \oplus E(\lambda_m, T)$.
- Suppose $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$ are distinct eigenvalues of $T$, and $V$ is finite-dimensional, then the sum of the dimensions of these eigenspaces is at most the dimension of $V$: $\dim E(\lambda_1, T) + \dots + \dim E(\lambda_m, T) \leq \dim V$
