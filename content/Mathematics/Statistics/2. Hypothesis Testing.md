Building on [[1. Sampling and Estimation]]: an estimator asks *"what is $\theta$?"*; a hypothesis test asks the sharper yes/no question *"is $\theta$ consistent with a specific claim?"*. Every test is built from a sample statistic whose sampling distribution we can pin down under that claim.

### The Framework

- A **parametric model** posits that the sample $X = (X_1,\dots,X_n)$ has joint distribution $P_\theta$ for some unknown $\theta$ in a **parameter space** $\Theta$.
- A **hypothesis** is a subset of $\Theta$. We split $\Theta$ into two disjoint pieces:
	- **Null hypothesis** $H_0: \theta \in \Theta_0$ — the default / status quo / "no effect".
	- **Alternative hypothesis** $H_1: \theta \in \Theta_1$ — the claim we are gathering evidence for.
	- A hypothesis is **simple** if its set is a single point (e.g. $\Theta_0 = \{\theta_0\}$) and **composite** otherwise (e.g. $\theta > \theta_0$).
- A **test** is a decision rule that maps the data to one of two verdicts: *reject $H_0$* or *do not reject $H_0$*. It is fully specified by a **rejection region** (**critical region**) $\mathcal{C}$ in the sample space — we reject $H_0$ exactly when the observed data falls in $\mathcal{C}$.
- In practice $\mathcal{C}$ is described through a **test statistic** $T = T(X)$: a statistic whose sampling distribution is *known under $H_0$*. We reject when $T$ lands in an extreme range that is improbable under $H_0$.

**Errors, size, and power.** A test can be wrong in two ways:

| | $H_0$ true | $H_0$ false |
|---|---|---|
| **Reject $H_0$** | Type I error (prob. $\alpha$) | correct (power) |
| **Do not reject** | correct | Type II error (prob. $\beta$) |

- **Type I error** (false positive): rejecting a true $H_0$. Its probability defines the **size** of the test,
	$$
	\alpha = \sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} P_\theta(\text{reject } H_0) = \sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} P_\theta(T \in \mathcal{C}).
	$$
	(For a simple null this is just $P_{\theta_0}(T\in\mathcal C)$.) The chosen bound $\alpha$ is the **significance level**, fixed in advance, e.g. $0.05$.
- **Type II error** (false negative): failing to reject a false $H_0$, with probability $\beta(\theta) = P_\theta(T \notin \mathcal C)$ for $\theta \in \Theta_1$.
- The **power function** is $\pi(\theta) = P_\theta(\text{reject } H_0)$. On the alternative, **power** $= \pi(\theta) = 1 - \beta(\theta)$; higher power means a test more sensitive to a true effect. Designing a test is choosing $\mathcal{C}$ to control size at $\alpha$ while maximizing power.
- The **$p$-value** is the smallest significance level at which the observed data would lead to rejection — equivalently, the probability under $H_0$ of a test statistic *at least as extreme* as the observed $t_{\text{obs}}$:
	$$
	p = P_{H_0}\big(T \text{ at least as extreme as } t_{\text{obs}}\big).
	$$
	**Decision rule:** reject $H_0$ iff $p \le \alpha$.

**The asymmetry, and a caveat on rigor.**
- The two hypotheses are treated *asymmetrically*: we fix and control the Type I error rate, so the null is "innocent until proven guilty". "Do not reject $H_0$" therefore means *insufficient evidence against $H_0$* — **not** "accept $H_0$".
- The definitions above are rigorous *given the model*, but two idealizations rarely hold exactly in practice:
	1. **$H_0$ and $H_1$ need not be exhaustive.** Rejecting $H_0$ is evidence for $H_1$ only by elimination within $\Theta$; it does not *prove* $H_1$.
	2. **The parameter space $\Theta$ itself is an assumption.** The real data-generating process may lie outside the assumed family $f(x\mid\theta)$ altogether, so "$\theta \in \Theta_0$" vs. "$\theta \in \Theta_1$" is a statement about the *model*, not raw reality. This is exactly the subtlety that goodness-of-fit testing runs into: a null of "the model fits perfectly" is essentially never literally true.

Two families of tests answer the framework's question. We first build up the **classical tests**, whose test statistics have an *exactly known* named null distribution, and then generalize to the **likelihood-based trinity**, which works for essentially any model asymptotically.

### Z-test

The simplest test comes straight from the sampling distribution of the sample mean. To test $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ when the population standard deviation $\sigma$ is **known**:

- Under $H_0$, $\bar{X}$ has mean $\mu_0$ and standard error $\sigma/\sqrt n$. Standardizing gives a statistic with a **standard normal** null distribution:
	$$
	Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \;\overset{H_0}{\sim}\; \mathcal{N}(0,1).
	$$
- This holds *exactly* if the population is normal, and *approximately* for any population with finite variance by the [[Mathematics/Probability/6. Limit Theorems|Central Limit Theorem]] — which is why the Z-test is associated with large samples.
- A **Z-test** is, generally, *any* test whose statistic is $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ under $H_0$. Rejection regions:
	- two-sided ($H_1:\mu\neq\mu_0$): reject if $|Z| > z_{1-\alpha/2}$;
	- one-sided: reject if $Z > z_{1-\alpha}$ (or $Z < -z_{1-\alpha}$).
- **Variations:** two-sample Z-test (difference of two means), Z-test for proportions.

### Student's t-test

Usually $\sigma$ is **unknown**. The natural fix is to plug in the sample standard deviation $S$ — but estimating $\sigma$ injects extra randomness into the denominator, so the standardized statistic is no longer normal:
$$
t = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{S/\sqrt{n}} \;\overset{H_0}{\sim}\; t_{\,n-1}.
$$
- The [[Mathematics/Probability/3. Common Distributions|Student's t distribution]] has heavier tails than the normal, accounting for that added uncertainty. A **t-test** is any test whose statistic is $t$-distributed under $H_0$.
- **Degrees of freedom** $=$ sample size minus the number of estimated parameters (here $n-1$, since $\bar X$ is estimated).
- As $n \to \infty$, $S \to \sigma$ and $t_{n-1} \to \mathcal{N}(0,1)$: the t-test *becomes* the Z-test. So the Z-test is the known-variance / large-sample limit of the t-test.
- **Variations:** two-sample (independent) t-test, paired t-test.

### F-test

To compare *variances* or test *several restrictions at once*, we need a statistic built from a ratio of variances. If $U_1 \sim \chi^2_{d_1}$ and $U_2 \sim \chi^2_{d_2}$ are independent, then by definition
$$
F = \frac{U_1/d_1}{U_2/d_2} \sim F(d_1, d_2).
$$
- An **F-test** is any test whose statistic is $F$-distributed under $H_0$. Because it is a ratio of (nonnegative) variances, $F > 0$; its shape depends on *two* degrees of freedom, numerator $d_1$ and denominator $d_2$.
- **Uses:** testing equality of two variances; **ANOVA**; and — most importantly in regression — testing a *joint* hypothesis that several coefficients are zero, by comparing the residual variance of a restricted vs. unrestricted model.
- **Key relationship:** a squared t-statistic is an F-statistic, $t_d^2 = F(1, d)$. So the t-test is the one-restriction special case of the F-test; the F-test is what you use when the null imposes more than one restriction.

The Z/t/F chain covers one or more *linear* restrictions on means under (asymptotic) normality. For general parameters and nonlinear models we need heavier machinery.

### The Holy Trinity of Likelihood Tests

For a model estimated by maximum likelihood, three general tests evaluate the evidence against $H_0$ from three geometric perspectives on the log-likelihood curve $\ell(\theta)$. Notation:

- $\hat\theta$ — the **unrestricted** MLE (maximizes $\ell$ globally);
- $\theta_0$ — the value fixed by $H_0$;
- $\tilde\theta$ — the **restricted** MLE (maximizes $\ell$ subject to $H_0$). If the model has several parameters, restricting one forces the others to shift to compensate, so $\tilde\theta \ne \hat\theta$ in general;
- $S(\theta) = \partial\ell/\partial\theta$ — the **score**; $I(\theta)$ — the **Fisher information**.

- **Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT)** — compares the *heights* of the log-likelihood. If $H_0$ is true, restricting the model should barely lower the maximized likelihood:
	$$
	LR = -2\big[\ell(\tilde\theta) - \ell(\hat\theta)\big].
	$$
	Requires estimating the model *twice* (restricted and unrestricted).
- **Wald Test** — measures the *horizontal distance* between $\hat\theta$ and $\theta_0$, scaled by the estimate's uncertainty:
	$$
	W = (\hat\theta - \theta_0)^T\,[\text{Var}(\hat\theta)]^{-1}\,(\hat\theta - \theta_0).
	$$
	Requires estimating *only the unrestricted* model, plus knowing $\text{Var}(\hat\theta) \approx I(\hat\theta)^{-1}$. (Only the components restricted by $H_0$ are compared.)
- **Lagrange Multiplier (LM) / Score Test** — measures the *slope* of $\ell$ at the restricted estimate. The score is zero at the global max $\hat\theta$; if $H_0$ is true, $\tilde\theta \approx \hat\theta$, so the slope at $\tilde\theta$ should be near zero:
	$$
	LM = S(\tilde\theta)^T\,I(\tilde\theta)^{-1}\,S(\tilde\theta).
	$$
	Requires estimating *only the restricted* model — cheapest when the restricted model is simple and the unrestricted one is complex.

**Relationships.**
- **The classical tests are special cases.** For a single mean, the Wald statistic is exactly $Z^2$ when $\sigma$ is known (and the squared-$t$/$F$ form when $\sigma$ is estimated): a two-sided Z-test *is* the one-parameter Wald test, since $Z^2 \sim \chi^2_1$.
- **Asymptotic equivalence.** As $n \to \infty$, $LR$, $W$, and $LM$ converge to the same value and, under $H_0$, all follow
	$$
	\chi^2_q, \qquad q = \text{number of restrictions imposed by } H_0.
	$$
	They yield the same asymptotic $p$-value.
- **Finite samples.** They generally differ. In the linear regression model there is a strict ordering $W \ge LR \ge LM$.
- **Which to use** is a matter of computational convenience: LM if only the restricted model is easy to fit, Wald if only the unrestricted one is, LR if both are available (often the most reliable).

### Goodness-of-Fit Tests

The tests above fix a family $f(x\mid\theta)$ and interrogate $\theta$. **Goodness-of-fit** tests instead interrogate the *family itself*: $H_0$ is "the data come from distribution $F$" (fully specified, or a family with estimated parameters), $H_1$ is "they do not".

- **Q-Q plot** (graphical). Plot the sample (empirical) quantiles against the theoretical quantiles of $F$, with a $45°$ reference line. Points on the line $\Rightarrow$ good agreement; systematic curvature/departure $\Rightarrow$ the shapes differ. A quick diagnostic, not a formal test.
- **Chi-square goodness-of-fit test** (binned data). Partition the range into $k$ bins, with **observed** counts $O_i$ and **expected** counts $E_i = n\,p_i$ under $H_0$. The statistic
	$$
	X^2 = \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i} \;\overset{H_0}{\approx}\; \chi^2_{\,k-1-p}
	$$
	(each term is an approximately squared-standard-normal deviation of a $\text{Bin}(n,p_i)$ count). Degrees of freedom $= k - 1 - p$, subtracting one for the constraint $\sum O_i = n$ and one for each of the $p$ parameters estimated from the data. Reject for large $X^2$.
- **Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test** (continuous $F$). Compare the empirical CDF $\hat F_n$ to $F$ via their largest vertical gap:
	$$
	D_n = \sup_{x} \big|\hat F_n(x) - F(x)\big|.
	$$
	- Under $H_0$, $\hat F_n \to F$ uniformly almost surely (**Glivenko–Cantelli theorem**), so $D_n \to 0$; reject for large $D_n$.
	- **Distribution-free.** Under $H_0$, $F(X_i) \sim U(0,1)$ (the probability integral transform), so the null distribution of $D_n$ does not depend on $F$ at all — one table (or one simulation) of critical values $d_\alpha$ serves every continuous $F$.

**A caveat: these are "badness-of-fit" tests.** For KS and chi-square, $H_0$ is "the fit is *perfect*". Two problems follow, echoing the rigor caveat from the framework above:
- *Failing to reject proves nothing* — it never establishes that $F$ is correct, only that the data are not blatantly incompatible with it.
- *Rejecting is unsurprising* — with enough data we can detect any imperfection, and no real process is *exactly* $F$; so we usually already "knew" $H_0$ was false.
- The honest question is not "is the fit perfect?" but "is $F$ a *good enough* approximation?". An **equivalence test** flips the hypotheses — $H_0$: "the fit is bad", $H_1$: "the fit is good enough (within a tolerance we must specify)" — so that *rejecting* $H_0$ delivers the positive conclusion we actually want. Formalizing this is awkward ($H_0$ becomes composite and the null distribution of the statistic is hard to derive), which is why it is often handled by simulation.

### Simulation-Based Testing

Every test above needs the null distribution of $T$. When that distribution is unknown or intractable (a complicated statistic, a composite/equivalence null, KS critical values for small $n$), we can *estimate* it directly: draw many datasets under $H_0$, recompute $T$ on each, and read the decision off the resulting collection of simulated values — either a critical value (a sample quantile of the simulated $T$'s) or a $p$-value (the fraction of simulated $T$'s at least as extreme as the observed $t_{\text{obs}}$). The $p$-value estimate is generally preferred: being a sample mean, it inherits a law-of-large-numbers guarantee. This is simply a Monte Carlo estimate of the null distribution of $T$.
