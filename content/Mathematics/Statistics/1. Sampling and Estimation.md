### The Sample

- We assume the observed data $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ are the realizations of random variables $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ generated by some underlying **data-generating process** (**DGP**).
- A **random sample** of size $n$ is a collection $X_1, \dots, X_n$ of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with common distribution $f(x \mid \theta)$.
	- $\theta$ is an unknown **parameter** (or vector of parameters) belonging to a **parameter space** $\Theta$.
	- The distribution $f(x \mid \theta)$ describes the **population**.
- We use uppercase $X_i$ for the random variables and lowercase $x_i$ for their observed realizations.

### Sample Statistics

- A **sample statistic** is any function $T = T(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ computed entirely from the sample, without reference to the unknown parameter $\theta$.
	- Because it is a function of random variables, a statistic is itself a random variable, with its own distribution called the **sampling distribution**.
	- Its observed value $t = T(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ is a single realization drawn from that sampling distribution.
- The entirety of inferential statistics is the study of these sampling distributions.
- The **order statistics** $X_{(1)} \le X_{(2)} \le \dots \le X_{(n)}$ are the sample values sorted in increasing order. $X_{(1)} = \min_i X_i$ and $X_{(n)} = \max_i X_i$.
- The **empirical cumulative distribution function** (ECDF) is the statistic
	$$
	\hat{F}_n(x) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{1}(X_i \le x),
	$$
	i.e. the fraction of the sample that is $\le x$. It is a step function that jumps by $1/n$ at each order statistic and is the sample analogue of the population CDF $F$.

### Estimators

- An **estimator** $\hat{\theta}$ (also written $\hat{\theta}_n$, for a sample of size $n$) is a sample statistic used to guess an unknown parameter $\theta$. The value obtained using the observed data is called the **estimate**.
- Since an estimator is a random variable, we judge it purely by properties of its sampling distribution. The following four properties are used to determine the "quality" of an estimator.

Biased vs Unbiased

- The **bias** is $\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = E(\hat{\theta}) - \theta$.
- An estimator $\hat{\theta}$ is **unbiased** if $E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta$ (the bias is zero), and **biased** otherwise.
- An estimator $\hat{\theta}$ is **asymptotically unbiased** if its bias goes to zero as the sample size goes to infinite, that is $\lim_{n\to\infty} E(\hat{\theta}_n) = \theta$.

Variance and Standard Error

- The **standard error** ($\text{SE}$) is the standard deviation of the estimator's sampling distribution. 
	- The term *standard deviation* may then be used to refer to the population standard deviation $\sigma$ without ambiguity. Then, $\sigma$ describes the spread of individual observations, $\text{SE}$ the spread of the estimator across repeated samples.
- Among unbiased estimators of the same $\theta$, the one with smaller variance (smaller standard error) is more **efficient**. Efficiency is a relative measure of precision.

Consistency

- An estimator $\hat{\theta}_n$ is **consistent** if it converges in probability to $\theta$, that is, for every $\epsilon>0$,
	$$
	\lim_{n\to\infty} P(|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta| \ge \epsilon) = 0.
	$$
- An estimator $\hat\theta_n$ is consistent if both its bias and its variance vanish, i.e. $\lim_{ n \to \infty } \text{Bias}(\hat\theta_n) = 0$ and $\lim_{ n \to \infty }\text{Var}(\hat\theta_n)=0$ (equivalently $\lim_{ n \to \infty } \text{MSE}(\hat{\theta_{n}})= 0$).
	- The converse is not true in general.

Mean Squared Error

- The **mean squared error** combines both sources of error (bias and variance) into one number:
	$$
	\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = E\big((\hat{\theta}-\theta)^2\big) = \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \big(\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})\big)^2
	$$
- **Bias–variance tradeoff**: reducing bias often increases variance and vice versa. A biased estimator can have a lower MSE than an unbiased estimator. Which estimator is preferable depends on $n$: variance typically shrinks with $n$, so for a persistently biased estimator an unbiased competitor eventually achieves lower MSE. This tradeoff only matters for fixed and finite $n$, asymptotically, any consistent estimator is ideal. 

The question remains on how to *construct* such an estimator $\hat\theta$. We consider three general approaches in the following sections: Method of Moments, Least Squares, and Maximum Likelihood Estimation.

### Method of Moments

- The **$k$-th (raw) sample moment** $M'_k = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^k$ is the sample analogue of the population moment $\mu'_k = E(X^k)$. In particular, the first raw moment is the **sample mean** $M'_1 = \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$.
- The **$k$-th central sample moment** $M_k = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^k$ is the sample analogue of the central population moment $\mu_k = E((X-\mu)^k)$.
- Each raw sample moment is itself a sample mean (of $X_i^k$), so by the law of large numbers it is a **consistent** estimator of the corresponding population moment, the central sample moments are consistent as well.
- The **Method of Moments** (**MoM**) writes the population moments as functions of $\theta$, sets them equal to the corresponding sample moments, and solves for $\theta$. With $p$ unknown parameters, the first $p$ moments suffice.
- MoM is simple and general, but the estimate depends on which moments are chosen and the method is often less efficient than maximum likelihood.

### Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Unlike the method of moments, maximum likelihood requires an assumption about the *family* of the probability distributions. We assume the data is generated by a known family $f(x \mid \theta)$, with only the parameter $\theta$ unknown.

- The **likelihood** is the joint density of the observed data $\mathbf{x}$ (a product, by independence), read as a function of $\theta$:
	$$
	L(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i \mid \theta)
	$$
- The **maximum likelihood estimate** $\hat\theta$ is the value of $\theta$ that maximizes $L(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})$, i.e. $\hat{\theta}$ is the parameter under which the observed data was most probable.
	- Problem: $L$ is a product of $n$ densities, each typically less than one, so it shrinks to astronomically small values for even moderate $n$ (numerical underflow), furthermore differentiating a long product is difficult.
- The **log-likelihood** is the log of the likelihood:
	$$
	\ell(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) = \log L(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log f(x_i \mid \theta).
	$$
- Since $\log$ is strictly increasing, $\ell$ has the *same* maximizer as $L$, but the product becomes a sum, which is generally more numerically stable and easier to differentiate. Consequently, MLE generally maximizes the log likelihood in practice.

Finding the MLE

- For models with strictly concave log-likelihoods (like most simple exponential family distributions), setting the first derivative of the log-likelihood to zero yields exactly one unique, global maximum.
- For complex, non-convex models, there may be multiple local maxima. Finding where the derivative is zero is insufficient, the true MLE is the global maximum among all local peaks.
- Because the derivative often cannot be solved algebraically, $\hat{\theta}$ is typically found using computational algorithms (like Gradient Descent). To avoid getting trapped in local maxima, these algorithms are often initialized from multiple random starting points.

Information and Theoretical Limit

- The **Fisher information** measures the curvature (the negative second derivative) of the log-likelihood around its maximum. A sharper peak means the data is highly sensitive to changes in $\theta$, meaning lower uncertainty:
    $$
    I(\theta) = -E\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ell(\theta|\mathbf{X})}{\partial\theta^2}\right]

### Common Statistics

A handful of statistics built from the sample moments do most of the everyday work, each is the natural estimator of a population quantity, produced by one of the methods above.

- The **sample mean** $\bar X = M'_1$ is an unbiased and consistent estimator of the population mean $\mu$ (by the law of large numbers), and has standard error $\text{SE}(\bar X)=\sigma/\sqrt n$. This means to decrease the standard error by a factor of $p$, you need to increase your sample size by a factor of $p^2$.
- The **sample median** is the middle order statistic ($X_{((n+1)/2)}$ for odd $n$, the average of the two central values for even $n$).
- The **sample variance** applies **Bessel's correction**, dividing by $n-1$ rather than $n$, to be exactly unbiased for $\sigma^2$:
	$$
	S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 = \frac{n}{n-1}M_2.
	$$
- The **sample standard deviation** is $S = \sqrt{S^2}$.
- The **sample skewness** $M_3/S^3$ measures the asymmetry of the data distribution around its mean.
- The **sample kurtosis** $M_4/S^4$ measures the tail heaviness (or extreme values) of the data distribution.
- The **sample covariance** $S_{XY}$ summarizes the joint variability of paired data $(X_i,Y_i)$ and is an unbiased estimator for the population covariance $\sigma_{XY}$:
	$$
	S_{XY} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar{X})(Y_i-\bar{Y})
	$$
- The **sample correlation** $R_{XY}$ is a unitless measure in $[-1,1]$ that standardizes the covariance to describe the strength and direction of the linear relationship between paired data:
    $$
    R_{XY} = \frac{S_{XY}}{S_X S_Y}
    $$


For multivariate data these statistics may be compactly expressed using matrix notation:

- **Data matrix**: an $n \times p$ matrix $X$ of $n$ observations on $p$ variables; entry $X_{ij}$ is variable $j$ of observation $i$.
- **Vector of sample means** ($p \times 1$): $\bar{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{n}X^T\mathbf{1}$, with $\mathbf{1}$ the $n\times 1$ vector of ones.
- **Centering matrix**: the symmetric idempotent $H = I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T$; then $HX$ subtracts each column's mean, centering the data.
- **Sample covariance matrix** ($p\times p$, unbiased for $\Sigma$): $\;S = \frac{1}{n-1}(HX)^T(HX) = \frac{1}{n-1}X^T H X$.
- **Scaling matrix**: $D = \text{diag}(S_1,\dots,S_p)$ of sample standard deviations.
- **Sample correlation matrix**: $R = D^{-1} S D^{-1}$, with unit diagonal.
