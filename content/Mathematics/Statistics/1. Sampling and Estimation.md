Statistics begins where [[Mathematics/Probability/index|probability]] ends. In probability we *assume* a distribution and reason about the data it produces; in statistics we *observe* the data and reason backwards about the distribution that produced it. The calculus and probability notes are assumed known throughout.

### The Sample

- We assume the observed data $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ are the realizations of random variables $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ generated by some underlying **data-generating process**.
- A **random sample** of size $n$ is a collection $X_1, \dots, X_n$ of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables drawn from a common distribution $f(x \mid \theta)$.
	- $\theta$ is an unknown **parameter** (or vector of parameters) belonging to a **parameter space** $\Theta$.
	- The distribution $f(x \mid \theta)$ (and hence $\theta$) describes the whole **population**; the sample is our finite window onto it.
- We use uppercase $X_i$ for the random variables (before the data is seen) and lowercase $x_i$ for their observed realizations (after).

### Sample Statistics

- A **sample statistic** is any function $T = T(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ computed entirely from the sample, without reference to the unknown parameter $\theta$.
	- Because it is a function of random variables, a statistic is *itself a random variable*, with its own distribution called its **sampling distribution**.
	- Its observed value $t = T(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ is a single realization drawn from that sampling distribution.
- The whole of inferential statistics is the study of these sampling distributions: an **estimator** (defined next) is a statistic aimed at a parameter, and a **test statistic** is a statistic whose sampling distribution under a hypothesis is known.
- The **order statistics** $X_{(1)} \le X_{(2)} \le \dots \le X_{(n)}$ are the sample values sorted in increasing order. $X_{(1)} = \min_i X_i$ and $X_{(n)} = \max_i X_i$.
- The **empirical (cumulative) distribution function** (ECDF) is the statistic
	$$
	\hat{F}_n(x) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{1}(X_i \le x),
	$$
	i.e. the fraction of the sample that is $\le x$. It is a step function that jumps by $1/n$ at each order statistic and is the sample analogue of the population CDF $F$.

### Estimators

- An **estimator** $\hat{\theta}$ (written $\hat{\theta}_n$ to emphasize sample size) is a sample statistic used to guess an unknown parameter $\theta$. The number obtained by plugging in observed data is an **estimate**.
- Since an estimator is a random variable, we judge it purely by properties of its sampling distribution. The four properties below are the vocabulary for "is this a good guess?"

**Bias and unbiasedness.**
- The **bias** is $\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = E(\hat{\theta}) - \theta$.
- $\hat{\theta}$ is **unbiased** if $E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta$ (zero bias): on average, across repeated samples, it hits the target.
- $\hat{\theta}$ is **asymptotically unbiased** if $\lim_{n\to\infty} E(\hat{\theta}_n) = \theta$.

**Variance and standard error.**
- The **standard error** ($\text{SE}$) is the standard deviation of the estimator's *sampling* distribution. It is conceptually distinct from the population standard deviation $\sigma$: $\sigma$ describes the spread of individual observations, $\text{SE}$ the spread of the estimator across repeated samples.
- Among unbiased estimators of the same $\theta$, the one with smaller variance is more **efficient**; efficiency is a relative measure of precision.

**Mean squared error.**
- The **mean squared error** combines both sources of error into one number:
	$$
	\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = E\big((\hat{\theta}-\theta)^2\big) = \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \big(\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})\big)^2
	$$
- **Bias–variance tradeoff**: reducing bias often increases variance and vice versa; a biased estimator can beat an unbiased one on MSE. Which wins usually depends on $n$: variance typically shrinks with $n$, so for a persistently biased estimator an unbiased competitor eventually achieves lower MSE. The tradeoff only matters at a fixed finite $n$; asymptotically, any consistent estimator is fine.

**Consistency (large-sample behavior).**
- $\hat{\theta}_n$ is **consistent** if it [[Mathematics/Probability/6. Limit Theorems|converges in probability]] to $\theta$: for every $\epsilon>0$,
	$$
	\lim_{n\to\infty} P(|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta| \ge \epsilon) = 0.
	$$
- **Sufficient condition:** $\hat\theta_n$ is consistent if both its bias and its variance vanish, i.e. $\lim_n \text{Bias}(\hat\theta_n) = 0$ and $\lim_n \text{Var}(\hat\theta_n)=0$ (equivalently $\text{MSE}\to 0$).
- Consistency is the minimal respectability requirement: with enough data the estimator lands on the truth.

### Estimation Methods

How do we *construct* the function $\hat\theta$? Three general recipes.

**Method of Moments (MoM).** The **$k$-th sample moment** $M'_k = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i^k$ is the sample analogue of the population moment $\mu'_k = E(X^k)$. MoM equates the first few population moments (functions of $\theta$) to the corresponding sample moments and solves for $\theta$. With $p$ unknown parameters, use the first $p$ moments. Simple and general, but often less efficient than MLE.

**Least Squares (minimizing squared error).** When the model is written as an *equation* — for a mean, $X_i = \mu + \varepsilon_i$ with errors $\varepsilon_i \sim \text{IID}(0,\sigma^2)$ — the least-squares estimate is the parameter value minimizing the sum of squared errors
$$
S(\mu) = \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu)^2.
$$
The first-order condition $\sum_i (X_i - \mu) = 0$ gives $\hat\mu = \bar X$: the sample mean is the least-squares estimator of the population mean. Dividing by $n$, this is equivalently the value minimizing the *mean* squared error $\frac1n\sum_i (X_i-\mu)^2$ — the empirical counterpart of $E[(X-\mu)^2]$, which is minimized at $\mu = E[X]$; so least squares is the "closest on average" principle. Like MoM it minimizes a distance function — but measured *directly in the data* rather than between sample and population moments — and like MoM it needs only the equation and its error structure, not a fully specified distribution (the requirement that distinguishes MLE below). Other loss functions yield other estimators: minimizing the sum of *absolute* errors $\sum_i |X_i-\mu|$ gives the sample median instead. Least squares is the workhorse of linear regression, where the constant $\mu$ is replaced by a regression function of explanatory variables.
> [!warning] Two different "MSE"s
> This construction criterion — squared error *minimized over candidate parameter values* — is distinct from the MSE of an estimator $\text{MSE}(\hat\theta)=E[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2]$ defined earlier to *evaluate* a given estimator across repeated samples. Here we minimize squared error to *produce* an estimator; there we compute it to *judge* one.

**Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE).** Choose the $\theta$ under which the observed data was most probable.
- The **likelihood** is the joint density of the sample viewed as a function of $\theta$: $L(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i \mid \theta)$ (product by independence).
- The **log-likelihood** $\ell(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sum_i \log f(x_i\mid\theta)$ is usually easier to maximize (sums, not products) and has the same maximizer.
- The MLE $\hat\theta$ solves the **score equation** $S(\theta) = 0$, where the **score** is the gradient of the log-likelihood:
	$$
	S(\theta) = \frac{\partial \ell(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})}{\partial \theta}.
	$$
- The **Fisher information** measures how sharply peaked the log-likelihood is (how much the data tells us about $\theta$):
	$$
	I(\theta) = -E\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial\theta\,\partial\theta^T}\right] = \text{Var}\big(S(\theta)\big).
	$$
- Under regularity conditions the MLE is **consistent, asymptotically normal, and asymptotically efficient**:
	$$
	\hat\theta_n \;\overset{d}{\approx}\; \mathcal{N}\!\big(\theta,\; I(\theta)^{-1}\big),
	$$
	attaining the smallest possible asymptotic variance. The score $S$ and information $I$ are also the backbone of the likelihood-based hypothesis tests.

### Sample Moments and Common Statistics

The general theory above applies to any estimator. In practice a handful of **sample moments** and their derivatives do most of the work; each is the natural estimator of a corresponding population quantity.

- The **$k$-th (raw) sample moment** estimates $\mu'_k = E(X^k)$: $\quad M'_k = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^k$.
- The **$k$-th central sample moment** estimates $\mu_k = E((X-\mu)^k)$: $\quad M_k = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^k$.

From these the workhorse statistics follow.

- The **sample mean** is the first raw moment $M'_1 = \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i$. It is unbiased ($E(\bar X)=\mu$) and consistent for $\mu$ by the WLLN, with $\text{SE}(\bar X)=\sigma/\sqrt n$.
- The **sample median** is the middle order statistic ($X_{((n+1)/2)}$ for odd $n$, the average of the two central ones for even $n$); more robust to outliers than the mean.
- The **sample variance** uses **Bessel's correction** (dividing by $n-1$) to be exactly unbiased for $\sigma^2$:
	$$
	S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 = \frac{n}{n-1}M_2.
	$$
	The central moment $M_2$ (dividing by $n$) is the biased-but-asymptotically-unbiased MLE of $\sigma^2$ — a textbook instance of the bias–variance discussion above.
- The **sample standard deviation** is $S = \sqrt{S^2}$.
- **Sample skewness** $M_3/S^3$ measures asymmetry; **sample kurtosis** $M_4/S^4$ measures tail heaviness.
- The **sample covariance** (unbiased for $\sigma_{XY}$) and **sample correlation** (unitless, in $[-1,1]$):
	$$
	S_{XY} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar{X})(Y_i-\bar{Y}), \qquad R_{XY} = \frac{S_{XY}}{S_X S_Y}.
	$$

#### Matrix Notation

For multivariate data these statistics are expressed compactly (see [[Mathematics/Linear Algebra/index|Linear Algebra]]).

- **Data matrix**: an $n \times p$ matrix $X$ of $n$ observations on $p$ variables; entry $X_{ij}$ is variable $j$ of observation $i$.
- **Vector of sample means** ($p \times 1$): $\bar{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{n}X^T\mathbf{1}$, with $\mathbf{1}$ the $n\times 1$ vector of ones.
- **Centering matrix**: the symmetric idempotent $H = I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T$; then $HX$ subtracts each column's mean, centering the data.
- **Sample covariance matrix** ($p\times p$, unbiased for $\Sigma$): $\;S = \frac{1}{n-1}(HX)^T(HX) = \frac{1}{n-1}X^T H X$.
- **Scaling matrix**: $D = \text{diag}(S_1,\dots,S_p)$ of sample standard deviations.
- **Sample correlation matrix**: $R = D^{-1} S D^{-1}$, with unit diagonal.
