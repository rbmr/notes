### Hypothesis Testing

- **Hypothesis testing** is a formal statistical procedure used to make decisions about the underlying probability distribution that generated a sample of data. 
- **Null Hypothesis** $H_0$: The default assumption, typically representing no effect, no difference, or the status quo.
- **Alternative Hypothesis** $H_1$ (or $H_a$): The claim we are gathering evidence to support.
- The null hypothesis $H_0$ and the alternative hypothesis $H_1$ are always disjoint (they cannot both be true at the same time).
- The null hypothesis $H_{0}$ and the alternative hypothesis $H_{1}$ are *not* exhaustive in general (if the null hypothesis is rejected, that does not guarantee the alternative hypothesis is true, the rejection of the null hypothesis is just evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis via process of elimination). 
- **Test Statistic**: A standardized random variable calculated from the sample data, used to determine whether to reject the null hypothesis. Because it is a function of the random sample, the test statistic has a known probability distribution under the assumption that $H_{0}$ is true.
- **Rejection Region (Critical Region)**: The range of values for the test statistic that are so unlikely (under $H_{0}$) that observing them leads to the rejection of $H_0$.
- **Type I Error** (False Positive): Rejecting $H_0$ when it is actually true.
	- Probability of Type I error: $\alpha = P(\text{Reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ is true})$.
	- $\alpha$ is also called the **Significance Level**. This is usually set in advance (e.g., $0.05$).
- **Type II Error** (False Negative): Failing to reject $H_0$ when $H_1$ is actually true.
	- Probability of Type II Error: $\beta = P(\text{Fail to reject } H_0 \mid H_1 \text{ is true})$.
- **Power of a test**: The probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis. A higher power indicates a more sensitive test that is better at detecting a true effect.
	$$
	\text{Power} = P(\text{Reject } H_0 \mid H_1 \text{ is true}) = 1 - \beta
	$$
- **$p$-value**: The probability of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one computed from the sample, assuming that the null hypothesis $H_0$ is absolutely true.
- Decision rule: If $p \leq \alpha$, the result is statistically significant; we reject $H_0$. If $p > \alpha$, we do not have enough evidence; we fail to reject $H_0$.

### Maximum Likelihood Tests

First we define the following:
- $\theta$ is the parameter(s) of interest,
- $\ell(\theta)$ is the log-likelihood function.
- $\hat{\theta}$ is the unrestricted Maximum Likelihood Estimate (the value that maximizes the likelihood globally),
- $\theta_{0}$ is the hypothesized value (the fixed, theoretical number proposed by the null hypothesis before looking at the data)
- $\tilde{\theta}$ is the restricted estimate (the value that maximizes the likelihood assuming $H_{0}$ is true)
- Note: If the model has multiple variables, restricting one parameter to $\theta_{0}$ forces the remaining parameters in $\tilde{\theta}$ to shift to compensate for the restriction, which is why $\tilde{\theta}$ is distinct from $\hat{\theta}$.

The following three tests are called the holy trinity of statistical tests, based on Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). They are asymptotically equivalent, and will generally lead to the same conclusion. However, they evaluate the evidence against the null hypothesis $H_{0}$ from three distinct perspectives on the log-likelihood curve.

- **Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT)**: compares the heights of the log-likelihood function.
	- Idea: measures how much the model's fit worsens when you restrict the parameters according to the null-hypothesis. If $H_{0}$ is true, restricting the model shouldn't reduce the likelihood by much.
	- Test statistic: $LR=-2[\ell(\tilde{\theta})- \ell(\hat{\theta})]$
	- This requires estimating the model twice, once under the null hypothesis (restricted) and once under the alternative (unrestricted). 
- **Wald Test**: Measures the horizontal distance on the parameter axis.
	- Idea: Evaluates how far the unrestricted estimate $\hat{\theta}$ is from the hypothesized value $\theta_{0}$ under $H_{0}$, relative to the uncertainty (variance) of the estimate. If $H_{0}$ is true, then the unrestricted estimate should not be far from the null value. 
	- Test statistic: $W = (\hat{\theta} - \theta_{0})^T[\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})]^{-1}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_{0})$.
		- Note since $\hat{\theta}$ and $\theta_{0}$ may have a different number of dimensions, $\hat{\theta}$ is restricted such that we only compare the matching parameters between the two. E.g. for $\hat{\theta}= (\hat{\mu}, \hat{\sigma}^2)^T$, and $\theta_{0}=\mu_{0}$, only compare $\hat{\mu}$ and $\mu_{0}$.
	- This requires estimating the model only once under the alternative hypothesis (unrestricted). It relies heavily on knowing the variance of the MLE. 
- **Lagrange Multiplier Test** (**LM Test / Score Test**): Evaluates the slope (gradient) of the log-likelihood function.
	- Idea: The derivative (score) of the log-likelihood function is zero at the global maximum $\hat{\theta}$. The LM test checks if the slope of the log-likelihood function at the restricted estimate $\tilde{\theta}$. If $H_{0}$ is true, $\tilde{\theta}$ should be very close to the true maximum, meaning that the slope at $\tilde{\theta}$ should be close to zero.
	- Test statistic: $LM=S(\tilde{\theta})^TI(\tilde{\theta})^{-1}S(\tilde{\theta})$, where $S(\theta)$ is the score function (first derivative of the log likelihood) and $I(\theta)$ is the Fisher information matrix.
	- It requires estimating the model only once, under the null hypothesis (restricted), which makes it highly computationally efficient when the restricted model is simple but the unrestricted model is complex.

As the sample size approaches infinity $n \to \infty$, the LRT, Wald and LM tests are mathematically equivalent. They will yield the exact same test statistic and $p$-value. Under the null hypothesis $H_{0}$, all three test statistics asymptotically follow a $\chi^2$ distribution. The degrees of freedom (the parameter of the $\chi^2$ distribution) is exactly equal to the number of restrictions imposed by the null hypothesis.

For finite (smaller) samples, the three tests will yield different different test statistics and consequent p values. For linear regression models, there is a strict mathematical inequality that dictates their behaviour: $M \geq LR \geq LM$.

### Z-test

A **Z-test** is any statistical hypothesis test in which the test statistic follows a standard normal distribution (Z-distribution) under the null hypothesis.

It is primarily used when the population standard deviation $\sigma$ is known. While traditionally associated with large sample sizes because the Central Limit Theorem guarantees the sample mean is approximately normally distributed, a Z-test can be used for small samples if the underlying population is definitively known to be normally distributed.

Test Statistic (One-sample Z-test): Evaluates whether a sample mean significantly differs from a hypothesized population mean.
$$
Z = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu_{0}}{\sigma/\sqrt{ n }}
$$

Common variations:
- Two-sample Z-test: compares the means of two independent populations to see if there is a significant difference between them.
- Z-test for proportions: used to test hypotheses about population proportions

### Student's t-test.

The **student's t-test** is any statistical hypothesis test in which the test statistic follows a Student's t-distribution under the null hypothesis. 

It is used when the population standard deviation $\sigma$ is **unknown** and must be estimated using the sample standard deviation $s$. It is especially critical for small sample sizes, though it remains mathematically valid and is frequently used for larger samples aswell.

The degrees of freedom parameter of the t-distribution is generally related to the sample size minus the number of estimated parameters.

Test statistic (One-sample t-test):
$$
t = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu_{0}}{s / \sqrt{ n }}
$$

Common variations:
- Independent (Two-sample) t-test: Compares the means of two independent groups to determine if they come from populations with equal means.
- Paired (Dependent) t-test: compares the means of two related groups or the same group evaluated at different times.

### F-test

An **F-test** is any statistical hypothesis test in which the test statistic follows an F-distribution under the null hypothesis.

It is primarily used to compare statistical models that have been fitted to a data set, identifying which model best fits the population from which the data were sampled. The test essentially evaluates the ratio of two different sources of variability (variances or mean squares).

Because it is a ratio of variances, an F-statistic is always positive. The shape of the F-distribution is determined by two separate degrees of freedom parameters: one for the numerator ($df_{1}$) and one for the denominator ($df_{2}$).
