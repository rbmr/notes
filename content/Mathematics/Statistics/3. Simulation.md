Components of the course that I deem statistics (Monte Carlo estimation, variance reduction, input analysis, output analysis) live under [[Mathematics/Statistics/index|Statistics]] instead.

A computer can only manufacture (pseudo)random *uniform* numbers, so everything reduces to one primitive: a stream of independent $U(0,1)$ draws. We first manufacture that stream, then transform it into whatever distribution we need — up to and including distributions too complicated for the elementary methods.

### Random Number Generation

- A **random number generator** (**RNG**) produces a sequence of numbers that cannot reasonably be predicted better than by chance.
- A **pseudorandom number generator** (**PRNG**) produces a sequence *deterministically* that nonetheless *appears* random. The goal is to emulate **independent** draws from a **uniform** $U(0,1)$ distribution.
	- Being deterministic with finite memory, a PRNG must eventually cycle. If $U_1, U_2, \dots$ is the output, there exist integers $P, i_0$ with $U_{i+P} = U_i$ for all $i \ge i_0$; the smallest such $P$ is the **period length**.

### Linear Congruential Generator

- A **Linear Congruential Generator** (**LCG**), proposed by Lehmer (1949), generates pseudorandom numbers by the recursion
	$$
	Z_{i+1} = (\alpha Z_i + c) \bmod m, \qquad U_{i+1} = Z_{i+1}/m,
	$$
	with integer parameters: seed $Z_0$, modulus $m$, multiplier $\alpha$, shift $c$.
	- The outputs are the fractions $\tfrac{0}{m}, \tfrac{1}{m}, \dots, \tfrac{m-1}{m}$, so the period satisfies $P \le m$. When $P = m$ the generator has **full period**.
- **Full Period Theorem.** The LCG has full period iff all of:
	- $c$ and $m$ are relatively prime;
	- $\alpha - 1$ is divisible by every prime factor of $m$;
	- $\alpha - 1$ is divisible by $4$ whenever $m$ is.
- **Classic examples:** MINSTD (Lewis–Goodman–Miller 1969): $m = 2^{31}-1$, $\alpha = 7^5$, $c=0$; SUPER-DUPER (Marsaglia 1972): $m = 2^{32}$, $\alpha = 69069$. Modern generators keep the recursive idea but are far more sophisticated.

### Random Variate Generation

With a uniform stream in hand, **random variate generation** turns $U(0,1)$ draws into samples from a target distribution. The four methods that follow are ordered roughly by generality: inverse transform, special properties, acceptance–rejection, and finally MCMC for the hardest cases.

### Inverse Transform Method

- **Theorem (probability integral transform).** Let $U \sim U(0,1)$ and let $F$ be a continuous, strictly increasing CDF. Then $X = F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$. (Conversely, if $X\sim F$ then $F(X)\sim U(0,1)$ — the fact that makes the KS test distribution-free.)
- **Method:** generate $U \sim U(0,1)$ and return $X = F^{-1}(U)$. The simplest method there is; applicability hinges on having an accurate formula for the (generalized) inverse CDF $F^{-1}$.

### Methods Using Special Properties

- **Key idea:** if the target $X$ can be written as $X = g(Y_1, \dots, Y_k)$ for random variables $Y_i$ we already know how to generate, then generate the $Y_i$ and return $X = g(Y_1,\dots,Y_k)$.
- The case $g(Y_1,\dots,Y_k) = Y_1 + \dots + Y_k$ is the **convolution method**.
- **Examples** (see [[Mathematics/Probability/3. Common Distributions|Common Distributions]] for the relationships):
	- $X \sim \text{Gamma}(n,\lambda)$, $n\in\mathbb N$: sum $n$ i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ draws.
	- $X \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$: with $Y_1 \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha,1)$, $Y_2 \sim \text{Gamma}(\beta,1)$, return $Y_1/(Y_1+Y_2)$.
	- $X \sim \chi^2_n$: sum of squares of $n$ i.i.d. $\mathcal N(0,1)$ draws.
	- $Z \sim t_n$: with $X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, $Y\sim\chi^2_n$, return $X/\sqrt{Y/n}$.

### Acceptance–Rejection Method

- **Idea:** draw from an easy density $g$ and use a random rule to decide whether to keep the draw as a sample of the target $f$.
- Find a constant $c$ with $f(x) \le c\,g(x)$ for all $x$; $t(x) = c\,g(x)$ is the **majorizing function**. The tightest (fewest rejections) constant is $c^* = \sup_x \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$.
- **Algorithm:**
	1. Generate $Y \sim g$.
	2. Generate $U \sim U(0,1)$ independent of $Y$.
	3. If $U \le \frac{f(Y)}{c\,g(Y)}$, accept and return $X = Y$; else reject and go to 1.
- **Efficiency:** the area under $f$ is $1$ and under $t$ is $c$, so each proposal is accepted with probability $1/c$. The tradeoff is keeping $c$ close to $1$ while keeping $g$ simple enough to sample.
- **Thinning** (non-homogeneous Poisson process with rate $\lambda(t)$): generate trial arrivals from a homogeneous process at the peak rate $\lambda^* = \max_t \lambda(t)$, then accept an arrival at time $t$ with probability $\lambda(t)/\lambda^*$.

### Markov Chain Monte Carlo

The methods above assume a "nice", usually low-dimensional target. For a **high-dimensional or very complicated** distribution — especially one known only *up to a normalizing constant*, e.g. the law of $(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ conditioned on some event — inverse transform is hopeless and acceptance–rejection becomes hopelessly inefficient (a good envelope needs a huge $c$, so almost everything is rejected).

- **MCMC idea:** instead of drawing independently, construct a **[[Mathematics/Probability/5. Stochastic Processes|Markov chain]]** whose long-run **stationary distribution** *is* the target. Run the chain; after it settles, its states are (dependent) draws from the target.
	- **Advantage:** copes with multi-dimensional / complicated targets.
	- **Disadvantage:** the draws are **dependent**, not i.i.d.

**Markov chain background.** A chain on states $1,\dots,N$ has transition matrix $P_{ij} = P(X_{t+1}=j \mid X_t=i)$. It is **irreducible** if every state is reachable from every other, **aperiodic** if it does not cycle rigidly, and **ergodic** if both. The **stationary distribution** $\{\pi_j\}$ is the unique solution of
$$
\pi_j = \sum_{i=1}^N \pi_i P_{ij}, \qquad \sum_j \pi_j = 1,
$$
interpreted as the long-run fraction of time in state $j$; for an ergodic chain it is also the limiting probability $\pi_j = \lim_{n\to\infty} P(X_n = j)$. The whole game is to build a $P$ whose $\{\pi_j\}$ equals the target $\{p_j\}$.

**Detailed balance.** A sufficient (and easy-to-arrange) condition: positive numbers $\{\pi_j\}$ summing to one satisfying the **detailed balance equation**
$$
\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji} \quad \forall\, i \ne j
$$
are automatically the stationary distribution. *Proof:* summing over $i$, $\sum_i \pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j \sum_i P_{ji} = \pi_j$, which is the stationarity equation. So it suffices to construct $P$ so that the target satisfies detailed balance — this is what the two algorithms below do.

**Metropolis–Hastings.** Start from *any* irreducible **proposal** $Q$ (entries $q_{ij}$), then accept a proposed move $i \to j$ with probability $\alpha_{ij}$, staying put otherwise. So $P_{ij} = q_{ij}\alpha_{ij}$ for $i\ne j$. Forcing detailed balance $\pi_i q_{ij}\alpha_{ij} = \pi_j q_{ji}\alpha_{ji}$ gives the **acceptance probability**
$$
\alpha_{ij} = \min\!\left\{\frac{\pi_j\, q_{ji}}{\pi_i\, q_{ij}},\ 1\right\}.
$$
- **The normalizing constant cancels.** If $\pi_j = b_j/B$ with $B$ unknown, then $\alpha_{ij} = \min\{b_j q_{ji}/(b_i q_{ij}),\,1\}$ — we only ever need the target *up to a constant*. This is MCMC's superpower.
- **Symmetric proposal (Metropolis):** if $q_{ij}=q_{ji}$, this collapses to $\alpha_{ij} = \min\{\pi_j/\pi_i,\,1\} = \min\{b_j/b_i,\,1\}$.
- **Continuous version:** proposals come from a conditional density $g(y\mid x)$ (e.g. $\mathcal N(x,\sigma^2)$); accept $y$ with $\alpha(y\mid x) = \min\{\,b(y)g(x\mid y)/(b(x)g(y\mid x)),\ 1\}$, again independent of $B$.
- **Algorithm:** initialize a proposal $Q$ (or $g$) and a starting state; repeatedly propose $Y$, draw $U\sim U(0,1)$, move if $U \le \frac{b_j q_{ji}}{b_i q_{ij}}$ (else stay), and record the state.

**Gibbs sampler.** A rejection-free special case that constructs $P = Q$ directly from the **full conditional** distributions. From the current $\mathbf x = (x_1,\dots,x_n)$:
1. Pick a coordinate $i \in \{1,\dots,n\}$ uniformly at random.
2. Draw $z$ from the full conditional $p_i(x_i \mid \mathbf x_{-i})$ of $X_i$ given all other coordinates.
3. Move to $\mathbf y = (x_1,\dots,x_{i-1}, z, x_{i+1},\dots,x_n)$ — **always accept**.

Detailed balance holds automatically: with $q(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x) = \tfrac1n\, p(\mathbf y)/p_{-i}(\mathbf x_{-i})$ and $\mathbf x,\mathbf y$ sharing $\mathbf x_{-i}$, one checks $p(\mathbf x)q(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x) = p(\mathbf y)q(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf y)$ — equivalently the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance probability equals $1$. Gibbs is ideal when the joint law is nasty but each *one-dimensional* conditional is easy to sample. *Example:* for independent $X_i\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda_i)$ conditioned on $\sum_i X_i > c$, the joint conditional is complicated, but by memorylessness each coordinate's conditional is just $\text{Exp}(\lambda_i)$ shifted by $\big(c - \sum_{j\ne i} x_j\big)^+$.

**Practical note — dependence and thinning.** Because successive states are correlated, to approximate i.i.d. draws one keeps only every $k$-th state ($X^k, X^{2k}, \dots$, e.g. $k=20$); the gaps loosen the dependence. Convergence to the stationary distribution is guaranteed only in the limit (via ergodicity), so early states are typically discarded.
