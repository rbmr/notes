### Monte Carlo Simulation

The core idea: *"I don't know how to calculate it directly, so I'll estimate it."* Named after the casino, the method was invented by Stanisław Ulam (1946, Los Alamos), who estimated a solitaire win probability by playing many random games rather than by combinatorics.

To estimate an unknown but **deterministic** quantity $\mu$:
1. Find a random variable $X$ that we can *sample from* with $E[X] = \mu$ and finite variance $\text{Var}(X) = \sigma^2 < \infty$.
2. Draw $n$ i.i.d. copies $X_1, \dots, X_n$.
3. Estimate $\mu$ by the sample mean $\bar X_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$.

The sample mean is an unbiased and consistent estimator, so its behavior is completely controlled by the limit theorems:
- **Why it works** — the [[Mathematics/Probability/6. Limit Theorems|Strong Law of Large Numbers]] gives $\bar X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} \mu$: the estimate converges to the truth.
- **How fast** — $\text{Var}(\bar X_n) = \sigma^2/n$, so the standard error shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$. Halving the error costs $4\times$ the samples.
- **Uncertainty quantification** — by the Central Limit Theorem, $\bar X_n \approx \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$, giving the approximate confidence interval
	$$
	\bar X_n \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}
	$$
	(with $\sigma$ replaced by the sample standard deviation $S$ in practice). Solving the half-width for $n$ tells you how many replications a target precision needs.

Two levers make a Monte Carlo estimator "good": it should be **cheap to sample** and have **low variance**. Sampling efficiency is a matter of the generation method; the variance we can attack directly, through *variance reduction*.

### Monte Carlo Integration

The most common use is estimating an integral that has no closed form, by rewriting it as an expectation.

1. Start from $\theta$ defined as an integral.
2. Rewrite it as $\theta = E[g(X)]$ for a random variable $X$ you can sample from. The standard trick is to factor the integrand into "a function $\times$ a density":
	$$
	\theta = \int_a^b h(x)\,dx = (b-a)\int_a^b h(x)\,\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{b-a}}_{U(a,b) \text{ density}}dx = (b-a)\,E[h(X)], \quad X \sim U(a,b).
	$$
3. Apply Monte Carlo: draw $X_1,\dots,X_n$, compute $g(X_i)$, and average. Since the $X_i$ are independent so are the $g(X_i)$, and
	$$
	\hat\theta_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n g(X_i)
	$$
	is unbiased ($E[\hat\theta_n]=\theta$) and consistent ($\hat\theta_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} \theta$ by the SLLN).

- **Example (1-D):** $\theta=\int_0^2 x^3\,dx = 2\,E[X^3]$ with $X\sim U(0,2)$, estimated by $\hat\theta_n = \frac{2}{n}\sum_i X_i^3$.
- **Example (multi-D):** $\theta = \int_0^2\!\int_0^3 g(x,y)\,dx\,dy = 6\,E[g(X,Y)]$ with $(X,Y)\sim U([0,3]\times[0,2])$, estimated by $\hat\theta_n = \frac{6}{n}\sum_i g(X_i,Y_i)$. Monte Carlo shines here: unlike grid-based quadrature, its $1/\sqrt n$ error rate is *independent of the dimension*.
- **Example (estimating $\pi$):** a point $(X,Y)$ uniform on $[-1,1]^2$ lands in the unit circle with probability $\tfrac{\text{area circle}}{\text{area square}} = \tfrac{\pi}{4}$. Writing this as an expectation of an indicator, $\pi = 4\,E[\mathbb 1(X^2+Y^2\le 1)]$, so $\hat\pi_n = \frac{4}{n}\sum_i \mathbb 1(X_i^2+Y_i^2\le 1)$. (Note that a *probability is just the expectation of an indicator* — an identity these Monte Carlo methods lean on repeatedly.)

### Bootstrapping

Monte Carlo estimates a quantity by sampling from a *known* distribution. **Bootstrapping** applies the same idea when the distribution we actually need is the *sampling distribution of an estimator* $\hat\theta = T(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ — the object required to attach a standard error or confidence interval to $\hat\theta$, but which we usually cannot derive in closed form.

- **Plug-in principle.** The obstruction is that the population $F$ is unknown. The bootstrap substitutes the best estimate of it we have — the empirical CDF $\hat F_n$ — and samples from *that*. Sampling from $\hat F_n$ is exactly **drawing the observed values with replacement**.
- **Nonparametric bootstrap** (standard error / confidence interval of $\hat\theta$):
	1. From the data $x_1,\dots,x_n$, draw a **bootstrap sample** $x_1^*,\dots,x_n^*$ of size $n$, *with replacement*.
	2. Recompute the statistic on it: $\hat\theta^* = T(x_1^*,\dots,x_n^*)$.
	3. Repeat $B$ times, giving replicates $\hat\theta^{*(1)},\dots,\hat\theta^{*(B)}$.
	4. Their empirical spread approximates the sampling distribution of $\hat\theta$: the **bootstrap standard error** is the sample standard deviation of the replicates, and a **percentile confidence interval** is the range between their $\alpha/2$ and $1-\alpha/2$ sample quantiles.
- **Parametric bootstrap.** If a parametric model is assumed, fit $\hat\theta$ once and generate the bootstrap datasets from the fitted model $f(x\mid\hat\theta)$ rather than by resampling the observed values.
- **Why it works** — two nested approximations, each vanishing in its own limit: $\hat F_n \to F$ as $n\to\infty$ (Glivenko–Cantelli, so resampling mimics the true data-generating process), and the Monte Carlo error from using finitely many replicates vanishes as $B\to\infty$. In effect the bootstrap trades an intractable analytic derivation for cheap computation.

### Hypothesis Testing via Simulation

A hypothesis test needs the distribution of the test statistic $T$ under $H_0$. When that distribution is unknown or intractable, we *simulate* it. Take a test that rejects $H_0$ when $T$ is large.

**Estimating the critical region** (via order statistics):
1. Draw i.i.d. datasets $X^1, X^2, \dots, X^n$ from $H_0$.
2. Compute the test statistic on each, $t_i := T(X^i)$.
3. Sort them; let $t_{(i)}$ be the $i$-th order statistic. The estimated critical region is the empirical $(1-\alpha)$ quantile and above:
	$$
	\hat{\mathcal C}_\alpha := \big[\,t_{(\lceil (1-\alpha)n\rceil)},\ \infty\,\big).
	$$
4. Reject $H_0$ if the observed $t_{\text{obs}} \in \hat{\mathcal C}_\alpha$.

**Estimating the $p$-value** (via a sample mean). Since a $p$-value is a probability, it is the expectation of an indicator — exactly a Monte Carlo target:
$$
p = P_{H_0}(T > t_{\text{obs}}) = E_{H_0}\big[\mathbb 1(T > t_{\text{obs}})\big].
$$
1. Draw datasets $X^1,\dots,X^n$ i.i.d. from $H_0$.
2. For each, compute $\mathbb 1(T(X^i) > t_{\text{obs}})$.
3. Estimate $\hat p_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb 1(T(X^i) > t_{\text{obs}})$, and reject if $\hat p_n \le \alpha$.

**Which is better?** The $p$-value estimate $\hat p_n$ is a *sample mean*, so the Law of Large Numbers gives it an asymptotic performance guarantee. The critical-region estimate is a *sample quantile*, which lacks the same clean guarantee here — so the $p$-value approach is generally preferred. (This same simulation recipe supplies the KS critical values and makes equivalence tests tractable.)

### Variance Reduction

Since a Monte Carlo estimator's precision is governed by $\text{Var}(\bar X_n) = \sigma^2/n$, we can either take more samples (costly, $1/\sqrt n$) or *reduce $\sigma^2$ itself* by constructing a cleverer estimator with the same mean. The lever is the covariance identity
$$
\text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y) + 2\,\text{Cov}(X,Y):
$$
if we can engineer a **negative** covariance, variance drops for free. All techniques below keep the estimator **unbiased** while shrinking its variance.

- **Antithetic variables.** To estimate $\theta = E[g(Z)]$ with $Z$ from a symmetric distribution (mean $\mu_Z$; typically $Z = U \sim U(0,1)$), pair each draw $Z_i$ with its mirror image $2\mu_Z - Z_i$ (for $U$: use $1-U_i$). Both have the same distribution, so
	$$
	\hat\theta_n^{a} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{g(Z_i) + g(2\mu_Z - Z_i)}{2}
	$$
	stays unbiased, with variance $\frac{1}{2n}\big[\text{Var}(g(Z)) + \text{Cov}(g(Z), g(2\mu_Z-Z))\big]$. This beats plain Monte Carlo whenever the covariance is negative — guaranteed when $g$ is **monotonic** (a small $U$ gives a large $1-U$, so the two evaluations move oppositely). Cost: two evaluations of $g$ per draw.
- **Control variates.** To estimate $\mu_Y = E[Y]$, exploit a correlated variable $X$ with *known* mean $\mu_X$. Since some of $Y$'s fluctuation is "explained" by $X$, correct for it:
	$$
	Y_c = Y + c\,(X - \mu_X), \qquad E[Y_c] = \mu_Y \text{ for any } c.
	$$
	The variance $\text{Var}(Y_c) = \text{Var}(Y) + c^2\text{Var}(X) + 2c\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)$ is minimized (a quadratic in $c$) at
	$$
	c^* = -\frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\text{Var}(X)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{Var}(Y_{c^*}) = \text{Var}(Y) - \frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)^2}{\text{Var}(X)} \le \text{Var}(Y).
	$$
	(Note the structural identity with the OLS slope.) *Any* nonzero correlation helps. In practice $c^*$ is estimated from the data, which reintroduces a small bias — remedied by estimating $c^*$ on one subsample and applying it on the rest.
- **Importance sampling.** To estimate $\theta = E_f[h(Z)] = \int h(x)f(x)\,dx$ when the "interesting" region of $h$ is rarely visited under $f$, sample from a better-targeted density $g$ and reweight by the **likelihood ratio** $w(x) = f(x)/g(x)$:
	$$
	\theta = \int h(x)\,\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\,g(x)\,dx = E_g[h(Z)\,w(Z)], \qquad \hat\theta_n^{IS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n h(Z_i)\,w(Z_i),\ \ Z_i \sim g.
	$$
	Unbiased, and variance-minimizing when $g(x) \propto |h(x)|f(x)$ (which would need $\theta$ itself, so in practice pick $g$ *shaped like* $h\cdot f$ — put samples where they matter).
- **Common Random Numbers (CRN).** When *comparing two configurations* (estimating $\mu_A - \mu_B$), drive both simulations with the *same* random numbers. This induces positive $\text{Cov}(A,B)$, and by $\text{Var}(A - B) = \text{Var}(A) + \text{Var}(B) - 2\,\text{Cov}(A,B)$ the positive covariance *reduces* the variance of the estimated difference. Intuition: comparing two systems on the same simulated "days" isolates the effect of the change from the noise of the environment. (Developed further alongside the machinery for comparing configurations later in the course.)
