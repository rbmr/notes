Graph Theory is the study of **graphs**, which are mathematical structures used to model the pairwise relationships between objects.

### Graphs

A **graph** $G$ is defined by a 2-tuple: $G=(V,E)$, where:
- Vertices $V$ (also called nodes), is the set of points in a graph.
- Edges $E$ is the set of connections between vertices. 
	- A graph is **undirected** if the edges are unordered pairs $\{ u, v \}$ representing bidirectional connections between vertices $u$ and $v$. 
	- A graph is **directed** if the edges are ordered pairs $(u,v)$ representing a one directional connection from a vertex $u$ (the **tail** vertex) to vertex $v$ (the **head** vertex). A directed graph is also called a **digraph**.

In many applications, it is useful to associate specific information (such as costs, probabilities, capacities, weights, labels, or colors) with the vertices or edges of a graph. 

### Self-Loops and Multiple Edges

- A **self-loop** is an edge from a vertex to itself.
- **Multiple edges** (also called **parallel edges**) are:
	- in an undirected graph: two or more edges that are incident to the same vertices.
	- in a directed graph: two or more edges with the same tail and head vertex.

We may categorize graphs on whether they allow self-loops and/or multiple edges.

| **Graph Types**        | **No Multiple Edges** | **Multiple Edges Allowed** |
| ---------------------- | --------------------- | -------------------------- |
| **No Self-loops**      | **Simple Graph**      | **Multigraph**             |
| **Self-loops Allowed** | **Loop Graph**        | **Pseudograph**            |

In general, the term "graph" refers to a simple graph.

### Empty Graphs

- An **empty graph** is a graph without any edges.
- A **trivial graph** is an empty graph with exactly one vertex.
- A **null graph** is an empty graph with zero vertices.
- A **complete graph** is a simple graph that contains all possible edges between its vertices. A complete graph with $n$ vertices is $K_{n}$.
	- Undirected: In $K_{n}$ every vertex has a degree of $n-1$. The total number of edges in $K_{n}$ is $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$.
	- Directed: In $K_{n}$ every vertex has a in and out degree of $n-1$. The total number of edges in $K_{n}$ is $n(n-1)$.
- A **semicomplete graph** is a directed graph where these is at least one directed edge between every pair of distinct vertices ($u \to v$, $v \to u$, or both).
- A **tournament** is a type of semicomplete graph where there exists exactly one directed edge between every pair of distinct vertices.

### Adjacency

- For any graph $G=(V,E)$, any edge $e \in E$ that has $u$ and $v$ as its endpoints is said to be an **incident edge of** or **incident to** both $u$ and $v$. If such an edge exists, $u$ and $v$ are said to be **adjacent**.
- For a directed graph $G=(V,E)$, any edge $( u,v )\in E$ is called an **outgoing edge** of $u$, an **incoming edge** of $v$. If such an edge exists, $u$ is said to be **adjacent to** $v$, and $v$ is said to be **adjacent from** $u$.
	- For an undirected graph, we dont distinguish between outgoing/incoming edges, or vertices being adjacent to/from another vertex.
- An **adjacency matrix** is a square $|V| \times |V|$ matrix used to represent a graph. If there exists and edge from vertex $i$ to vertex $j$, the matrix entry $A_{i,j}$ is $1$. Otherwise, it is $0$.
	- For an undirected graph, this matrix is always symmetric across its diagonal.
- An **adjacency list** is a list of $|V|$ elements, where each index $i$ stores a list all vertices $j$ for which an edge exists from vertex $i$ to vertex $j$.
	- For an undirected graph, $v$ appears in $u$'s list, iff $u$ appears in $v$'s list.

### Degree

- The **degree** of a vertex $v$ (denoted $d(v)$ or $deg(v)$) is the number of incident edges of $v$. Formally $d(v)=|\{ e \in E \mid e \text{ is incident to } v \}|$.
- The **Handshaking Lemma** states that the sum of all degrees equals two times the number of edges: $\sum_{v \in V} d(v) = 2|E|$.

For directed graphs we further distinguish between the out-degree and the in-degree.

- The **out-degree** of a vertex $v$ (denoted $d^+(v)$, or $deg^+(v)$) is the number of outgoing edges of $v$. Mathematically: $d^+(v) = |\{ (v, u) \in E \mid u \in V \}|$
- The **in-degree** of a vertex $v$ (denoted $d^-(v)$ or $deg^-(v)$) is the number of incoming edges of $v$. Mathematically: $d^-(v) = |\{ (u, v) \in E \mid u \in V \}|$
- Consequently:
	- For any vertex $v\in V$, $d(v) = d^{+}(v) + d^-(v)$
	- $\sum_{v \in V} d^+(v) = \sum_{v \in V} d^-(v) = |E|$.

### Paths

The standard definitions used in graph theory are as follows:

- A **walk** of **length** $k$ is a finite sequence $v_0, v_1, \dots, v_k \in V$, such that for all $0 \leq i < k$, there is an edge from $v_{i}$ to $v_{i+1}$.
	- A walk is **closed** if $v_{0} = v_{k}$, and **open** otherwise.
	- A walk is **trivial** if $k=0$. If a walk this trivial, then this is stated explicitly.
	- For an undirected graph, any walk $v_{0}, v_{1}, \dots, v_{k}$ can be reversed to get another walk $v_{k}, v_{k-1}, \dots, v_{0}$.
- A **trail** is a walk in which no edge is traversed more than once. 
- A **circuit** is a closed trail.
- A **path** is a walk in which all vertices are distinct. 
	- Mathematically: For all $0 \leq i < j \lt k$, $v_i \neq v_j$.
	- Every path is also a trail, as repeating an edge would require repeating its incidents.
- A **cycle** is a closed path.

Note that the use of these terms is inconsistent across literature. The following is an overview of each of the standard terms and their properties, accompanied by an alternative name.

| Standard Term | Distinct | Closed | Alternative Name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Open Walk | None | No | Path |
| Closed Walk | None | Yes | Cycle |
| Trail | Edges | No | - |
| Circuit | Edges | Yes | - |
| Path | Vertices | No | Simple Path |
| Cycle | Vertices | Yes | Simple Cycle |

Interesting properties:

- If $A$ is the adjacency matrix of a graph $G$ (directed or undirected) with vertices $\{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}$, then for any positive integer $k$: the entry in the $i$-th row and $j$-th column of the matrix $A^k$ is equal to the number of walks of length $k$ from vertex $v_{i}$ to vertex $v_{j}$.
- Per definition, any cycle is a circuit, and any circuit is a closed walk. Furthermore any closed walk can be reduced to a cycle by removing a sub-sequence of vertices. Consequently, for any graph $G$ the following statements are equivalent:
	- $G$ contains a closed walk. 
	- $G$ contains a circuit.
	- $G$ contains a cycle.

### Cycles and Connectedness

Definitions

- A graph is called **cyclic** if it contains a cycle and **acyclic** otherwise.
- An undirected graph is **connected** if for every pair of vertices $u$ and $v$ there exists a path between $u$ and $v$.
- A directed graph is called **strongly connected** if for every pair of vertices $u$ and $v$, there exists a path from $u$ to $v$ AND a path from $v$ to $u$. A directed graph is called **weakly connected** if its underlying undirected graph is connected.

Properties for Undirected Graphs

- Any acyclic undirected graph has at most $|V|-1$ edges, and at most one path between any two vertices.
- Any connected undirected graph has at least $|V| - 1$ edges, and at least one path between any two vertices.
- A **tree** is an undirected graph that is both acyclic and undirected. Consequently, any tree has exactly $|V| - 1$ edges, and exactly one path between any two vertices.
- A **forest** is an undirected graph that is acyclic. 
	- A forest can be understood as a disjoint union of zero or more trees. 
	- Any tree is a forest.

Properties for Directed Graphs

-  A **topological ordering** is a linear ordering of vertices in a directed graph $G=(V,E)$ such that for any directed edge $(u, v) \in E$, vertex $u$ comes before vertex $v$ in the ordering. 
- A **directed acyclic graph** (**DAG**) is a graph that is both directed and acyclic.
	- A graph has a topological ordering of its vertices if and only if it is a DAG.

### Rooted Trees

- A **rooted tree** is a tree with exactly one vertex $r \in V$ chosen as the root. This implies a natural directional hierarchy. For any two adjacent vertices $u$ and $v$ where $u$ lies on the unique path from the root to $v$:
	- $v$ is called a **child** of $u$
		- a vertex with no children is called a **leaf** (or **external node**)
		- a vertex with at least one child is called an **internal node**.
	- $u$ is called the **parent** of $v$
		- two vertices with the same parent are called **siblings**
- The **depth** of a vertex $v$ in a rooted tree is the length of the unique path from the root $r$ to $v$. The **height** of a rooted tree is the maximum depth among all vertices in $V$. The height of a single vertex tree is $0$.
- An **ordered tree** is a rooted tree where the children of each vertex have an explicit linear ordering. In visual representations, this ordering is usually depicted from **left to right**.
- A **$k$-ary tree** is a rooted tree where every internal vertex has at most $k$ children.
- A **full $k$-ary tree** (also called a strictly $k$-ary tree) is a $k$-ary tree where every vertex has either exactly $0$ or $k$ children. 
- A **perfect $k$-ary tree** is a full $k$-ary tree where all leaf nodes are located at the exact same depth. 
	- A perfect $k$-ary tree of height $h$ always contains exactly $\frac{k^{h+1}-1}{k-1}$ total vertices.
- A **complete $k$-ary tree** of height $h$ is an ordered $k$-ary tree where every depth level $d$ (for $0 \leq d < h$) is maximally filled with $k^d$ vertices, and all vertices at the deepest level $h$ are concentrated as far left as possible.
	- Every perfect $k$-ary tree is a complete $k$-ary tree, but not all complete $k$-ary trees are perfect.
