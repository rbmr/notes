### Preliminary Knowledge

This note assumes familiarity with entropy, relative entropy, and mutual information ([[1. Entropy and Information Measures]]), and the PDF of the Gaussian and Uniform distributions ([[3. Common Distributions]]).

### Differential Entropy

- For a continuous random variable $X$ with PDF $f(x)$, the **differential entropy** is defined by direct analogy with the discrete sum, replaced by an integral:
	$$
	h(X) = -\int f(x)\log f(x)\,dx
	$$
- Differential entropy behaves *qualitatively* differently from discrete entropy, because a PDF is a density, not a probability:
	- **It can be negative.** Example: $X\sim\text{UNIF}(0,a)$ has $f(x)=\frac1a$ on $(0,a)$, so $h(X) = -\int_0^a \frac1a\log\frac1a\,dx = \log a$, which is negative whenever $a<1$.
	- **It is not invariant under (even one-to-one) transformations of $X$**, unlike discrete entropy. For $Y=aX$ ($a>0$), the transformation rule for densities gives $f_Y(y) = \frac1a f_X(y/a)$, and substituting into the definition shows $h(Y) = h(X)+\log a$: rescaling the outcome variable shifts the differential entropy, whereas relabeling the outcomes of a discrete variable never changes its (discrete) entropy.
	- It is still translation-invariant: $h(X+c) = h(X)$ for any constant $c$, since shifting a density doesn't change its shape.
- **Connection back to discrete entropy**: quantizing $X$ into bins of width $\Delta$ (rounding $X$ down to the nearest multiple of $\Delta$) produces a discrete random variable $X_\Delta$ with entropy approximately $H(X_\Delta) \approx h(X) - \log\Delta$ for small $\Delta$ (each bin has probability $\approx f(x)\Delta$, and expanding $-\sum f(x)\Delta\log(f(x)\Delta)$ recovers this). As $\Delta\to 0$ this diverges to $+\infty$ — a continuous variable takes infinitely many bits to describe exactly — but the *offset* $h(X)$ is exactly the finite part left over, which is why it remains a useful quantity despite not being an "amount of information" in the same sense as discrete entropy.
- **Relative entropy and mutual information carry over unchanged**, replacing sums by integrals: $D(f\|g) = \int f\log\frac fg$ and $I(X;Y) = \iint f(x,y)\log\frac{f(x,y)}{f(x)f(y)}\,dx\,dy$. Crucially, both are still non-negative (Gibbs' inequality still applies — the discrete proof in [[1. Entropy and Information Measures#Relative Entropy]] goes through unchanged with sums replaced by integrals), and both are *invariant* under one-to-one transformations of $X$ or $Y$: the $-\log\Delta$ divergence introduced by quantizing (or the $\log a$ shift from rescaling) appears identically in both the numerator and denominator density and cancels. This is why the capacity results below are stated in terms of mutual information rather than differential entropy directly.

### Maximum-Entropy Distributions

- **Among all continuous distributions on a bounded interval $[a,b]$, the uniform distribution maximizes differential entropy.** Let $f$ be any density on $[a,b]$ and $g$ the uniform density $\frac1{b-a}$ on $[a,b]$.
	1. By Gibbs' inequality (continuous version), $D(f\|g)\ge 0$, i.e. $\int f\log f \ge \int f\log g$.
	2. Since $g$ is constant ($\frac1{b-a}$) on the support of $f$, $\int f\log g\,dx = \log\frac1{b-a}\int f\,dx = \log\frac1{b-a} = -h(g)$.
	3. Combining, $-h(f) = \int f\log f \ge -h(g)$, i.e. $h(f) \le h(g)$.
- **Among all continuous distributions on $\mathbb R$ with a fixed variance $\sigma^2$, the Gaussian maximizes differential entropy.** Let $f$ be any density with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and $g$ the $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ density.
	1. Again by Gibbs' inequality, $-h(f) = \int f\log f \ge \int f\log g$.
	2. Since $g$ is Gaussian, $\log g(x) = -\frac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$ — a quadratic function of $x$. So $\int f(x)\log g(x)\,dx$ depends on $f$ *only* through its mean and variance (the coefficients multiplying $1$, $x$, and $x^2$ inside the quadratic), which by assumption match those of $g$ exactly.
	3. Hence $\int f\log g\,dx = \int g\log g\,dx = -h(g)$ (replacing $f$ by $g$ in step 2's integral changes nothing, since only the matching moments mattered).
	4. Combining: $-h(f) \ge -h(g)$, i.e. $h(f) \le h(g)$.
	- Direct computation gives $h(g) = \frac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)$ for the Gaussian itself.
- Both proofs follow the identical pattern: non-negativity of relative entropy, applied against a candidate distribution whose log-density is built only from the constrained moments.

### The Gaussian Channel

- The **Gaussian channel** models a continuous input $X$ corrupted by independent Gaussian noise: $Y=X+Z$, $Z\sim N(0,N)$, together with an average **power constraint** on the input, $E[X^2]\le P$ (without a power constraint, the capacity would be infinite — arbitrarily large input values could encode arbitrarily fine distinctions).
- The channel capacity (in bits per use) is
	$$
	C = \max_{p(x):\,E[X^2]\le P} I(X;Y) = \frac12\log\left(1+\frac PN\right)
	$$
	1. $I(X;Y) = h(Y)-h(Y\mid X)$. Since $Y=X+Z$ with $Z$ independent of $X$, conditioned on $X=x$ the only randomness left in $Y$ is $Z$ itself, so $h(Y\mid X) = h(Z) = \frac12\log(2\pi e N)$ (the differential entropy of $N(0,N)$, independent of the choice of $p(x)$).
	2. So maximizing $I(X;Y)$ over input distributions is equivalent to maximizing $h(Y)$ alone.
	3. $Y=X+Z$ has variance $\text{Var}(X)+N \le P+N$ (using $E[X^2]\le P$ and independence of $X,Z$). By the maximum-entropy result above, $h(Y)$ is maximized, subject to a variance constraint, when $Y$ is Gaussian — and $h(Y)$ is increasing in $Y$'s variance (from the explicit formula $\frac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2)$), so the best achievable is $h(Y) = \frac12\log(2\pi e(P+N))$, attained by making $Y$ Gaussian with the largest allowed variance $P+N$.
	4. Since $Z\sim N(0,N)$ is fixed, $Y=X+Z$ is Gaussian with variance $P+N$ exactly when $X\sim N(0,P)$ (sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian, variances add) — so this maximum is achievable, not just an upper bound.
	5. Putting it together: $C = h(Y)-h(Z) = \frac12\log(2\pi e(P+N)) - \frac12\log(2\pi e N) = \frac12\log\frac{P+N}{N} = \frac12\log\left(1+\frac PN\right)$.
- $P/N$ is the **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)**. The formula makes the qualitative behavior explicit: capacity grows without bound as $N\to 0$ (noiseless channel), and $\to 0$ as $N\to\infty$ or $P\to 0$ (signal drowned out or absent).
