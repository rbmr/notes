### Preliminary Knowledge

This note assumes familiarity with entropy and the chain rule for entropy ([[1. Entropy and Information Measures]]), and discrete-time Markov chains together with their stationary distributions ([[5. Markov Chains#Limiting behaviour]]).

### Entropy Rate

- For a stochastic process $\{X_i\}$, the joint entropy $H(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$ generally grows with $n$. To measure how much *new* uncertainty each additional symbol contributes on average, define the **entropy rate**:
	$$
	H(\mathcal X) = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n H(X_1,\ldots,X_n)
	$$
	when this limit exists. A process is **stationary** if the joint distribution of any window $(X_{k+1},\ldots,X_{k+n})$ does not depend on the starting index $k$ (only on the window length $n$); stationarity is assumed for the rest of this note, and guarantees the limit above exists.
- An equivalent, and often more useful, definition of the entropy rate uses conditional entropy instead of a per-symbol average:
	$$
	H'(\mathcal X) = \lim_{n\to\infty} H(X_n\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{n-1})
	$$
	i.e. the uncertainty in the next symbol, given the entire past, in the long run.
- **These two definitions agree**: $H(\mathcal X) = H'(\mathcal X)$ for any stationary process.
	1. By the entropy chain rule, $H(X_n\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{n-1})$ is non-increasing in $n$: conditioning on more of the past can only reduce (average) uncertainty about the next symbol (conditioning reduces entropy, see [[1. Entropy and Information Measures#Mutual Information]]), and by stationarity $H(X_n\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{n-1})$ depends only on $n$, not on where the window starts.
	2. A non-increasing sequence bounded below by $0$ converges, so $H'(\mathcal X) = \lim_{n\to\infty}H(X_n\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{n-1})$ exists.
	3. By the chain rule, $\frac1n H(X_1,\ldots,X_n) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n H(X_i\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{i-1})$: the per-symbol average entropy is exactly the Cesàro mean (running average) of the conditional entropy sequence from step 1.
	4. The Cesàro mean of a convergent sequence converges to the same limit as the sequence itself. Hence $H(\mathcal X) = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n H(X_1,\ldots,X_n) = H'(\mathcal X)$.
- Intuition: eventually, "the average uncertainty over the whole sequence so far" and "the uncertainty in the very next symbol" must coincide — if they didn't, the running average would still be trending, contradicting convergence.

### Entropy Rate of a Markov Chain

- For a stationary Markov chain (time-homogeneous, started from its stationary distribution $\varpi$), the definition via conditional entropy collapses to a single step, since the Markov property means only the immediately preceding symbol matters: $H(X_n\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{n-1}) = H(X_n\mid X_{n-1})$ for all $n\ge 2$.
- Therefore the entropy rate is just the conditional entropy of one transition, averaged over the stationary distribution:
	$$
	H(\mathcal X) = H(X_2\mid X_1) = -\sum_i \varpi_i \sum_j P_{ij}\log P_{ij}
	$$
	where $P_{ij}$ are the transition probabilities and $\varpi$ is the stationary distribution ($\varpi = \varpi P$). Each row of the transition matrix contributes its own entropy $-\sum_j P_{ij}\log P_{ij}$ (the uncertainty in "where do I go next, given I'm in state $i$"), weighted by how often the chain is in state $i$ in the long run.
- Example: an i.i.d. process ($P_{ij} = p(j)$ for every row $i$, i.e. the next state doesn't actually depend on the current one) recovers $H(\mathcal X) = H(X)$, the ordinary entropy of a single symbol — consistent with there being no structure across time to reduce the per-symbol rate below that.
