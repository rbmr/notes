### Preliminary Knowledge

This note assumes familiarity with joint, marginal, and conditional distributions ([[4. Multivariate Random Variables]]) and Jensen's inequality ([[Jensen's Inequality]]).

Throughout, $\log$ denotes $\log_2$ unless stated otherwise, so information is measured in **bits**. Using a different base only rescales every quantity in this note by the same constant factor. By convention $0\log 0 = 0$ (justified by $\lim_{p\to 0^+} p\log p = 0$), so events of probability zero never contribute.

### Self-Information

- The **self-information** (or **surprisal**) of an outcome $x$ with probability $p(x) > 0$ is $I(x) = -\log p(x) = \log\frac{1}{p(x)}$.
- Properties:
	- $I(x) \ge 0$, with $I(x) = 0$ exactly when $p(x)=1$ (a certain outcome carries no information).
	- $I(x)$ is strictly decreasing in $p(x)$: rarer outcomes are more surprising, hence carry more information.
	- If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $I(x,y) = I(x)+I(y)$: the surprisal of two unrelated facts is additive. This is why a logarithm appears at all — it is the (essentially unique) function turning multiplication of independent probabilities into addition of information.

### Entropy

- The **entropy** (or **Shannon entropy**) of a discrete random variable $X$ with PMF $p(x)$ is the expected self-information:
	$$
	H(X) = E[I(X)] = -\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)
	$$
	Intuition: entropy is the average number of bits of "surprise" $X$ generates — a measure of how unpredictable $X$ is.
- Examples:
	- A fair coin ($p=\frac12$ for each outcome): $H(X) = -2\cdot\frac12\log\frac12 = 1$ bit.
	- A certain outcome ($p(x_0)=1$): $H(X)=0$, matching the intuition that a deterministic variable is maximally predictable.
	- Uniform on $n$ outcomes: $H(X) = -\sum_{i=1}^n \frac1n\log\frac1n = \log n$.
- **Entropy is maximized by the uniform distribution.** For $X$ supported on a finite set of size $n$, $H(X) \le \log n$, with equality if and only if $X$ is uniform on those $n$ values.
	1. Write $H(X) = E\left[\log\frac{1}{p(X)}\right]$, the expectation taken over the (at most $n$-point) support of $X$.
	2. Since $\log$ is concave, Jensen's inequality gives $E\left[\log\frac{1}{p(X)}\right] \le \log E\left[\frac1{p(X)}\right]$.
	3. Expand the right-hand expectation: $E\left[\frac1{p(X)}\right] = \sum_{x:p(x)>0} p(x)\cdot\frac1{p(x)} = |\text{support}(X)| \le n$.
	4. Combining, $H(X) \le \log n$. Equality in Jensen's inequality requires $\frac{1}{p(X)}$ to be almost surely constant, i.e. $p(x)$ the same for every $x$ in the support, with the support of size exactly $n$ — that is, $X$ is uniform.

### Joint and Conditional Entropy

- The **joint entropy** of $X$ and $Y$ is the entropy of the pair, treated as a single random variable: $H(X,Y) = -\sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log p(x,y)$.
- The **conditional entropy** of $Y$ given $X$ averages the entropy of $Y$'s conditional distribution over the possible values of $X$:
	$$
	H(Y\mid X) = \sum_x p(x) H(Y\mid X=x) = -\sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log p(y\mid x)
	$$
	Intuition: the remaining uncertainty in $Y$ once $X$ is known.
- **Chain rule**: $H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y\mid X)$.
	1. By definition of conditional probability, $p(x,y) = p(x)p(y\mid x)$, so $\log p(x,y) = \log p(x) + \log p(y\mid x)$.
	2. Substitute into the joint entropy: $H(X,Y) = -\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\big[\log p(x)+\log p(y\mid x)\big] = -\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log p(x) \;-\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log p(y\mid x)$.
	3. The first sum depends on $x$ only through $\sum_y p(x,y) = p(x)$, giving $-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)=H(X)$. The second sum is $H(Y\mid X)$ by definition.
	- By symmetry, also $H(X,Y) = H(Y)+H(X\mid Y)$.
	- This generalizes to $n$ variables: $H(X_1,\ldots,X_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n H(X_i\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{i-1})$, applying the two-variable chain rule repeatedly, treating $(X_1,\ldots,X_{i-1})$ as a single joint random variable at each step.

### Relative Entropy

- The **relative entropy** (or **Kullback-Leibler divergence**) between two PMFs $p$ and $q$ on the same set is
	$$
	D(p\|q) = \sum_x p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}
	$$
	(with $0\log\frac0q = 0$, and $D(p\|q)=\infty$ if $p(x)>0=q(x)$ for some $x$). It measures how distinguishable $q$ is from the "true" distribution $p$, from the point of view of an observer sampling from $p$. It is not a metric: it is generally asymmetric, $D(p\|q) \ne D(q\|p)$.
- **Gibbs' inequality**: $D(p\|q) \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $p=q$.
	1. Restrict the sum to $x$ with $p(x)>0$ (other terms contribute $0$), and write $D(p\|q) = -\sum_{x:p(x)>0} p(x)\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} = -E_p\left[\log\frac{q(X)}{p(X)}\right]$.
	2. Since $\log$ is concave, Jensen's inequality gives $E_p\left[\log\frac{q(X)}{p(X)}\right] \le \log E_p\left[\frac{q(X)}{p(X)}\right]$.
	3. Expand: $E_p\left[\frac{q(X)}{p(X)}\right] = \sum_{x:p(x)>0} p(x)\cdot\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} = \sum_{x:p(x)>0} q(x) \le \sum_x q(x) = 1$.
	4. So $E_p[\log(q(X)/p(X))] \le \log 1 = 0$, hence $D(p\|q) = -E_p[\log(q(X)/p(X))] \ge 0$. Equality in step 2's Jensen requires $q(X)/p(X)$ a.s. constant on the support of $p$, and equality in step 3 requires that support to already cover all of $q$'s mass; together these force $p=q$.

### Mutual Information

- The **mutual information** between $X$ and $Y$ is the relative entropy between their joint distribution and the product of their marginals:
	$$
	I(X;Y) = D\big(p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)p(y)\big) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}
	$$
	Intuition: how much knowing $Y$ tells you about $X$ (and vice versa). It is $0$ exactly when $X$ and $Y$ are independent, since then $p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)$ makes every term of the sum vanish.
- **Mutual information decomposes entropy.** Expanding $\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)} = \log\frac{p(x\mid y)}{p(x)} = -\log p(x) - \big(-\log p(x\mid y)\big)$ and taking expectations term by term gives three equivalent expressions:
	$$
	I(X;Y) = H(X)-H(X\mid Y) = H(Y)-H(Y\mid X) = H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)
	$$
	(the last form follows from the chain rule $H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y\mid X)$ substituted into the middle expression).
- **Non-negativity**: since $I(X;Y)$ is itself a relative entropy, Gibbs' inequality immediately gives $I(X;Y) \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are independent.
- **Corollary — conditioning cannot increase entropy on average**: from $I(X;Y) = H(X)-H(X\mid Y) \ge 0$,
	$$
	H(X\mid Y) \le H(X)
	$$
	with equality if and only if $X\perp Y$. This is a statement about *average* uncertainty: for a specific observed value $Y=y$ it is entirely possible that $H(X\mid Y=y) > H(X)$ (learning one specific fact can make you more confused), but averaged over all values of $Y$, uncertainty cannot go up.

### Data Processing Inequality

- Random variables $X,Y,Z$ form a **Markov chain**, written $X\to Y\to Z$, if $Z$ is conditionally independent of $X$ given $Y$: $p(x,z\mid y) = p(x\mid y)p(z\mid y)$. Intuitively, all influence of $X$ on $Z$ is routed through $Y$.
- **Data processing inequality**: if $X\to Y\to Z$, then $I(X;Z) \le I(X;Y)$. No processing of $Y$ (deterministic or random) can increase the information it contains about $X$.
	1. Expand the joint mutual information $I(X;Y,Z)$ using the chain rule for mutual information twice, in two different orders: $I(X;Y,Z) = I(X;Y)+I(X;Z\mid Y) = I(X;Z)+I(X;Y\mid Z)$. (The chain rule for mutual information follows the same algebra as the entropy chain rule, replacing entropies by mutual informations.)
	2. Since $X\to Y\to Z$, $p(x\mid y,z) = p(x\mid y)$, so $X$ and $Z$ are independent given $Y$, making $I(X;Z\mid Y) = 0$.
	3. Substituting into the first expansion: $I(X;Y,Z) = I(X;Y)$.
	4. Since mutual information is non-negative, $I(X;Y\mid Z) \ge 0$, so from the second expansion: $I(X;Z) = I(X;Y,Z) - I(X;Y\mid Z) \le I(X;Y,Z) = I(X;Y)$.
